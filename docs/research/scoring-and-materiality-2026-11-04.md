**Evidence quality.** Where I say "reported" it means a paper measured it; where I say "I infer" it is my extrapolation to Choir. Most LLM-judge literature is on chat/QA preference tasks, not on judging probabilistic predictions of consequences; transfer is by analogy.

**Two findings that change the design doc, up front** (details in sections 1 and 2):
1. The PSA doc's `C*` is "built from what independent critics surface and from what actually happened", and `w` includes `relevance`. A weight that depends on the realized outcome makes the score improper: this is exactly the "forecaster's dilemma" (Lerch et al., 1512.09244). Weights must be fixed ex ante (from the decision problem and pre-registered reference predictors), with "what actually happened" used only to *grow the reference set for future commitments*, never to reweight the settled score. This is the single most important constraint on problem (A).
2. The agent's prediction `P(c|a)` is conditioned on its own action, and the action is chosen using the prediction. Plain proper scoring of the realized branch is not incentive-compatible for action choice (decision scoring rules; performative prediction). A clean fix exists and costs something Choir already half-has: randomize a small fraction of actions with logged propensities (the 10% held-out set) and score with inverse-propensity weights.

## 1. Proper scoring rules and their limits

### 1.1 What the literature establishes
- **Brier 1950; log score (Good 1952); spherical; CRPS (Matheson and Winkler 1976).** Strictly proper rules make honest reporting uniquely optimal in expectation. Canonical modern treatment: Gneiting and Raftery 2007, "Strictly proper scoring rules, prediction, and estimation," JASA 102, doi:10.1198/016214506000001437 [verified]. Also Savage 1971 JASA; Bröcker and Smith 2007, "Scoring probabilistic forecasts: the importance of being proper," Weather and Forecasting [verified].
- **Decomposition.** Murphy 1973, "A new vector partition of the probability score," J. Applied Meteorology 12 [verified]: Brier = reliability (calibration) - resolution + uncertainty. Reliability and resolution are separate skills; a constant base-rate forecaster is perfectly calibrated with zero resolution. DeGroot and Fienberg 1983 on calibration/refinement [verified: The Statistician 32]. This is the literature behind the PSA doc's "calibration can improve simply by becoming vaguer."
- **Skill scores are improper in general.** Brier skill score against a reference is a ratio of expected scores and is, with finite samples and a fitted/varying reference, not proper (Murphy 1973; Mason 2008 [corrected: the reference-strategy paper is Mason 2004, "On using climatology as a reference strategy in the Brier and ranked probability skill scores", Monthly Weather Review 132; no 2008 paper was checked]). The bayesian-trust doc's `q_i` = "Brier skill against the base rate, clipped at 0" inherits this: clipping at 0 discards the negative tail and makes the aggregate incentive non-proper (a forecaster is indifferent to how badly they lose below zero). Prefer unclipped proper score differences (score minus reference score, summed), and clip only at the trust-view layer.
- **Prequential principle.** Dawid 1984, "Statistical theory: the prequential approach," JRSS A [verified: JRSS A 147(2), 1984]. The PSA doc already cites its spirit.

### 1.2 Limits that matter here
1. **Weighted scoring and the forecaster's dilemma.** Lerch, Thorarinsdottir, Ravazzolo, Gneiting 2017, "Forecaster's Dilemma: Extreme Events and Forecast Evaluation," Statistical Science, arXiv:1512.09244 [V-api]. Evaluating forecasts only on (or weighting by) realized extreme events rewards forecasters who always predict extremes; conditioning on the outcome destroys propriety. The proper alternatives weight by *the forecast-independent region of the outcome space* via threshold- or quantile-weighted scores: Gneiting and Ranjan 2011, "Comparing density forecasts using threshold- and quantile-weighted scoring rules," JBES [verified: JBES 29(3), 2011; arXiv id still unchecked]; Holzmann and Klar 2017, "Focusing on regions of interest in forecast evaluation," Annals of Applied Statistics [verified: 11(4), 2017]. **Transfer:** direct. Weights must be a function of the *question/decision*, fixed before outcomes. **Non-transfer:** those papers weight a continuous outcome axis; Choir's `c` is a categorical consequence set, so the analogue is weighting each typed question by an ex-ante weight, which is already how `TypedQuestion.Weight` works (good) as long as it is frozen at commit time.
2. **Choquet / Schervish representation: every proper rule is a mixture of cost-loss decision problems.** Schervish 1989, "A general method for comparing probability assessors," Annals of Statistics [verified: Ann. Statist. 17(4), 1989]; Ehm, Gneiting, Jordan, Krüger 2016, "Of quantiles and expectiles: consistent scoring functions, Choquet representations, and forecast rankings," JRSS B, arXiv:1503.08195 [V-api]; Buja, Stuetzle, Shen 2005 "Loss functions for binary class probability estimation" [unverifiable: unpublished manuscript, not located]. A binary proper score is an integral over decision thresholds `t` of the regret of the cost-loss decision "act iff p > t"; Brier corresponds to a uniform mixing measure over thresholds, log score to a measure that blows up at 0 and 1. **This is the formal bridge from "score materiality, which is decision-relative" to proper scoring:** choosing a materiality weighting *is* choosing the mixing measure over decision thresholds of the downstream decider (the owner's gate threshold, tau). A claim whose decision threshold is never near its predicted probability has near-zero weight, which is the PSA doc's `relevance` = "would flip the verdict," derived rather than asserted.
3. **Coherence of cost assumptions in classifier metrics.** Hand 2009, "Measuring classifier performance: a coherent alternative to the AUC," Machine Learning [verified]; Hernández-Orallo, Flach, Ferri 2012, "A unified view of performance metrics: translating threshold choice into expected classification loss," JMLR [verified: JMLR 13]. AUC implicitly uses different cost distributions for different classifiers, so it is not a coherent comparison. **Transfer:** if each agent or class is scored with a different implicit weighting (e.g., weights derived by the agent's own critics), cross-agent comparisons are incoherent. Use one governance-set mixing measure per action class.
4. **Conditional on the agent's own action: decision scoring rules.** Oesterheld and Conitzer 2020, "Decision Scoring Rules," WINE 2020 [web: DBLP-listed, no arXiv] [verified: DBLP lists the paper in WINE 2020]; precursors Othman and Sandholm 2010 "Decision rules and decision markets" (AAMAS) and Chen and Kash 2011 "Information elicitation for decision making" (AAMAS) [verified]; Hanson's futarchy as the motivation. The problem: forecasts of outcome-given-action are used to *choose* the action, and only the chosen branch is observed. Scoring only the realized branch with a proper rule lets the forecaster profit by influencing which branch is chosen (e.g., by shading the forecast of a branch to steer the decision toward a branch that is easier to predict or already known). The known fix is to randomize the decision with positive probability on each action, score the realized branch with inverse-propensity weighting (as in off-policy evaluation), and show the result is proper in the decision-conditional sense. **Cost:** exploration noise, a fraction of actions made not because they are best. **Mapping:** Choir already has a 10% held-out set (precommitment-records 1.0). If the held-out set also randomizes action choice among admissible alternatives with logged propensity, the decision-scoring-rule machinery applies to those cases. Without randomization, there is no proper way to score counterfactual branches that were never taken; they must be treated as unsettled, not scored.
5. **Performative prediction.** Perdomo, Zrnic, Mendler-Dünner, Hardt 2020, "Performative Prediction," ICML, arXiv:2002.06673 [V-api]. Predictions change the distribution they are evaluated on; "performatively stable" points are fixed points of retraining, not optima. Oesterheld, Treutlein, Cooper, Hudson 2023, arXiv:2305.17601 [V-web, UAI 2023] [verified: abstract matches, including the 5-10% simulation errors]: score-maximizing reports under performativity generally do not reflect the expert's true beliefs; bounds on the distortion; for binary outcomes with bounded influence scoring rules can make optimal reports arbitrarily close to belief, impossible for more than two outcomes; simulations suggest errors often above 5-10%; performative stability incentivizes the belief-reflecting fixed points. **Transfer:** Choir predictions ("this edit will not break X") are visibly performative (the prediction gates authorization, which changes the action). **Failure mode reported:** the distortion is not small in toy settings. The PSA doc already excludes questions "whose outcome is the emitter's own later act"; the literature says that exclusion is necessary but not sufficient, because the *gate* creates performativity for every prediction that can block an action. **Design response:** (a) separate "forecast-for-calibration" (settled, scored) from "forecast-that-gates"; (b) score only stable/consistent reports; (c) for gating predictions, score on the decision-score-rule path with randomization or accept the bias and bound it with the published bounds.
6. **Self-fulfilling and self-defeating predictions.** Same family as 5; classic statement in the forecasting literature that proper scoring assumes exogenous outcomes. No Choir-specific empirical literature found. Weak evidence either way beyond the toy simulations above.
7. **Hedging and vagueness.** Proper rules reward the honest probability, not informativeness; informativeness shows in resolution and in the choice of question. The PSA doc's specificity field and skill vs. base-rate are the right tools; the literature gap is that no standard score rewards "chose a question worth asking," which is the materiality problem itself (section 7).
8. **Proper rules on discrete, many-outcome claims with LLM-produced probabilities.** LLM verbalized probabilities are typically coarse and overconfident (section 3.5 on Jev's own measured miscalibration); a proper rule applied to a rounded 0.0/1.0 distribution with log score has unbounded loss, so clamp (the PSA doc already clamps). Brier is bounded and more forgiving of coarse outputs; log is more sensitive to rare-event omissions. For materiality of rare catastrophes (which is where log's penalty for assigning 0 matters) prefer log with a floor, and report both.

### 1.3 What transfers / what does not
- Transfers: propriety, the Murphy decomposition, one-score-per-question accounting, prequential discipline, weighting-by-threshold (Schervish), ex-ante-weights-only (Lerch).
- Does not transfer: any proof that assumes outcomes are exogenous to the report; anything assuming a known outcome space (the consequence space `C*` is open-ended and constructed).
- Reported failure modes: improper skill scores; outcome-conditioned weights; hedged/constant forecasts that are calibrated but uninformative; performative distortion; unbounded log loss.

## 2. Value of information and decision-relevant scoring

### 2.1 Literature
- **EVPI/EVOI.** Raiffa and Schlaifer 1961, Applied Statistical Decision Theory [unverifiable: book not checked]; Howard 1966, "Information value theory," IEEE Trans. Systems Science and Cybernetics [verified]. EVPI = expected payoff with perfect information minus best payoff without, an upper bound on any EVOI. Felli and Hazen 1998 "Sensitivity analysis and the expected value of perfect information," Med Decis Making [verified: Med Decis Making 18(1), 1998] for partial EVPI.
- **Decision-theoretic and utility-weighted scoring.** Hendrickson and Buehler 1971 "Proper scores for probability forecasters" [verified: Ann. Math. Statist. 42(6)]; Schervish 1989 (above) for the cost-loss mixture; Vickers and Elkin 2006, "Decision curve analysis: a novel method for evaluating prediction models," Med Decis Making [verified: 26(6), 2006]: *net benefit* at a threshold probability `pt` = TP/N - FP/N * pt/(1-pt), reported across a range of thresholds. Decision curve analysis is the applied, clinical form of "score a prediction by its value to the decider at the decider's threshold," and is the closest existing practice to Choir's `relevance` weight. Its reported limit: it needs a stated threshold range, which is the same as needing a stated decision problem.
- **Relevance as decision-flip probability.** I found no paper that defines "relevance = probability a piece of information flips the decision" and uses it to weight forecast scores. The nearest constructs: EVPI/EVSI (value of information is zero if no information can change the optimal action: this is a theorem, not a heuristic; Howard 1966); probabilistic sensitivity analysis and "decision-flip" analyses in health economics (Claxton 1999, "The irrelevance of inference," J Health Economics [verified: 18(3), 1999], argues decisions should depend on expected value, with research value from EVPI); Itti and Baldi 2009 "Bayesian surprise attracts human attention," Vision Research [verified: 49(10), 2009] (surprise as KL from prior to posterior). So `relevance(c)` as flip-probability has strong theoretical backing (VOI = 0 when no flip) but I found no empirical validation in AI-agent setups.
- **Counterfactual evaluation of stated alternatives.** Off-policy evaluation: Dudík, Langford, Li 2011, "Doubly robust policy evaluation and learning," ICML, arXiv:1103.4601 [verified]; Swaminathan and Joachims 2015 [unverifiable: not checked]. These estimate what a policy would have gotten from logged data with known propensities. Requires logged propensities and overlap (positivity). **Transfer:** gives the machinery for scoring "alternatives considered but not taken" only if actions were randomized; otherwise causal inference from observational traces (Pearl; Hernán and Robins) is needed and is brittle.
- **LLM-specific.** FActScore (Min et al. 2023, arXiv:2305.14251 [V-api]) and LongFact/SAFE (Wei et al. 2024, arXiv:2403.18802 [V-api]) decompose long text into atomic facts and weigh each equally; they do not weight facts by importance, and the community treats this as a known limitation (my reading; not a measured failure). The summarization Pyramid method (Nenkova and Passonneau 2004 [verified: the pyramid method; a 2007 TSLP version was located, the 2004 NAACL original was not]) weights content units by how many human summarizers included them: an *annotator-consensus importance weight*, which is a usable template for graded importance without a ground-truth utility.

### 2.2 Failure modes reported or implied
- Threshold dependence: any decision-relative score is only as good as the stated decision problem (decision curve analysis literature).
- VOI is subjective-prior relative: EVPI computed under the agent's own beliefs is endogenous and can be inflated by the agent. Use reference priors.
- Counterfactual evaluation without logged propensities is unidentified; critics' imagined alternatives are not data.

### 2.3 What transfers
Define `relevance(c)` operationally as: probability, under a *reference predictor*, that learning `c` changes the *authorizer's* verdict (gate/no gate, or choose a different alternative). Compute via EVOI-style simulation: re-run the decision policy (rule or LLM authorizer) with and without `c` revealed; flip rate = relevance. Estimating it needs the authorizer to be a function that can be called counterfactually: management/gate policies in Choir are, so this is cheap relative to the open-ended problem. Its dependency on a reference distribution over `c` is the main residual (the "reference set is the weak link" in PSA 3.1).

## 3. Meta-scoring without ground truth

### 3.1 Latent-variable truth inference (Dawid-Skene lineage)
- Dawid and Skene 1979, "Maximum likelihood estimation of observer error-rates using the EM algorithm," JRSS C [verified: Applied Statistics 28(1), 1979]. Per-rater confusion matrices; EM jointly estimates true labels and rater reliabilities.
- Raykar et al. 2010 JMLR "Learning from crowds" [verified]; Whitehill et al. 2009 GLAD (item difficulty plus rater ability) [verified: NIPS 2009]; Hovy et al. 2013 MACE [verified: NAACL 2013]; Karger, Oh, Shah 2011/2014, "Budget-optimal task allocation for reliable crowdsourcing systems," arXiv:1110.3564 [V-api]; Zhang, Chen, Zhou, Jordan 2014, "Spectral methods meet EM," arXiv:1406.3824 [V-api] (consistency of Dawid-Skene via spectral initialization; requires conditional independence of raters given the true label); Zheng et al. 2017 "Truth inference in crowdsourcing: is the problem solved?" VLDB [verified: PVLDB 10(5), 2017] (benchmark: no method dominates [verified: "no method performs consistently the best"]; simple majority voting is often near the best; gains over it are modest on real data [claim not found in source: the paper reports that D&S, BCC and LFC perform better than other methods in decision-making and single-label tasks, and treats MV only as a baseline; do not cite it for "MV is near the best"]: the first part is the measured result worth citing).
- Weak supervision: Ratner et al. 2016 data programming (arXiv:1605.07723 [verified]) and Snorkel (arXiv:1711.10160 [V-api]) learn the accuracies of noisy labeling functions without ground truth, again via conditional-independence (or an estimated dependency structure).
- **Independence requirement and its failure:** all of these need raters to be conditionally independent given truth (or a modeled dependency graph). **Transfer problem for Choir:** LLM scorers share training data, architectures, and prompts: errors are positively correlated, so unsupervised agreement statistics overestimate accuracy and cannot detect shared blind spots. No paper I know measures conditional dependence of LLM judges in Dawid-Skene terms; the self-preference and shared-bias papers in 3.5 are indirect evidence that independence fails. I flag this as an *open and empirically testable* question, not a settled one.

### 3.2 Peer prediction and Bayesian Truth Serum
- Miller, Resnick, Zeckhauser 2005, "Eliciting informative feedback: the peer-prediction method," Management Science [verified: 51(9), 2005]: pay a rater by a proper score of the predicted peer report; truth-telling is a Bayes-Nash equilibrium given a common prior known to the mechanism.
- Prelec 2004, "A Bayesian truth serum for subjective data," Science [verified: 306(5695), 2004]: reward answers that are "surprisingly common" relative to the crowd's predictions; needs large populations, no mechanism-known prior. Prelec, Seung, McCoy 2017, "A solution to the single-question crowd wisdom problem," Nature [verified: 541, 2017] (surprisingly-popular). Witkowski and Parkes 2012 robust BTS [verified: AAAI 2012]; Radanovic and Faltings [unverifiable: no specific paper named].
- Multi-task: Dasgupta and Ghosh 2013, arXiv:1303.0799 [V-api] (effort/endogenous proficiency); Shnayder, Agarwal, Frongillo, Parkes 2016, "Informed truthfulness in multi-task peer prediction," arXiv:1603.03151 [V-api]; Kong and Schoenebeck 2019, "An information theoretic framework for designing information elicitation mechanisms that reward truth-telling," ACM TEAC, arXiv:1605.01021 [V-api]; Kong 2020, "Dominantly truthful multi-task peer prediction with a constant number of tasks," arXiv:1911.00272 [V-api].
- **Key negative result:** Gao, Wright, Leyton-Brown 2016, "Incentivizing evaluation via limited access to ground truth: peer-prediction makes things worse," arXiv:1606.07042 [V-web] [verified against the abstract]. Peer-prediction mechanisms admit equilibria where raters skip costly effort and coordinate on cheap signals; the problem is unavoidable when agents can coordinate via cheap signals; a simple mechanism that spot-checks against limited ground truth and otherwise pays unconditionally gives stronger incentive guarantees with less ground-truth access than many peer-prediction mechanisms. **This is the strongest single piece of literature for Choir's architecture:** use a *small, randomized, unpredictable ground-truth spot-check* to anchor, not pure peer agreement.
- Learned/automated peer prediction: Liu and Chen 2017 "Machine-learning aided peer prediction" (EC) [verified: EC 2017].
- **Independence requirements, summarized:** MRZ needs a common prior and conditionally independent signals; BTS needs large populations and a common-prior assumption; multi-task variants need task-exchangeability and rater signal independence across tasks; all are vulnerable to collusion on uninformative signals (Gao et al.). LLM scorers violate "signals are conditionally independent given the world" because they share a pretraining distribution, and they make "cheap signal" collusion trivial (e.g., all say "confirmed" on surface plausibility).
- **LLM-era evidence, new and weak but directly on point.** Robertson and Koyejo 2025, "Let's Measure Information Step-by-Step ..." arXiv:2508.05469 [V-web] [verified: AUC 0.70-0.77 under attack; judges near 0.50 in the paper body]: evaluation by estimated mutual information between responses with TVD-based (f-divergence) estimator; report AUC 0.70-0.77 under adversarial attack where ordinary judge queries sit near chance (~0.50) (self-reported; preregistration claimed by authors). Qiu, Carroll, Allen 2026, "Truthfulness Despite Weak Supervision: Evaluating and Training LLMs Using Peer Prediction," arXiv:2601.20299 [V-web, ICLR 2026] [verified: ICLR 2026; 5-20x, 0.135B and the inverse-scaling claim match the abstract]: peer-prediction reward via mutual predictability; claims an "inverse scaling" property (more robust to deception as the capability gap widens), LLM-as-judge reported worse than random against deceptive models 5-20x the judge's size, and a 0.135B model usable as reward source. Both are 2025-26, few independent replications; treat as promising, not established. They matter because they are the first evidence that peer-style scoring can hold where judge-style scoring collapses, and because they specifically address adversarial agents.

### 3.3 Surrogate scoring
- Liu, Wang, Chen 2020, "Surrogate Scoring Rules," EC / ACM TEAC, arXiv:1802.09158 [V-api]: score a report against a noisy peer/reference label by correcting for the reference's noise rates, so the expected score equals a proper score against ground truth when the noise rates are known or estimated. Dominant-strategy truthful with known confusion matrix; the reference's noise must be independent of the reporter's. **Transfer:** this is the cleanest formalization of "score against a cheap noisy scorer (System 1), then debias it." The noise rates are what the Jev audit sample estimates. It also means "treat scorer probabilities as a monotone score until calibrated locally" (PSA 5.3) has a theory. **Non-transfer:** assumes noise is label-independent given class (class-conditional); LLM judge noise depends on content (verbosity, position, self-preference), so the correction is approximate.

### 3.4 Gold items, honeypots, test-retest, prediction-powered inference
- Gold/honeypot items: Snow, O'Connor, Jurafsky, Ng 2008, "Cheap and fast, but is it good?" EMNLP [verified: EMNLP 2008] (non-expert labels near expert quality after aggregation, with spot-check calibration); crowdsourcing quality-control practice (embedded gold). Failure: gold items that are recognizable as gold get gamed; they must be indistinguishable in distribution from live items (Gao et al. spot-check argument).
- **Prediction-powered inference (PPI).** Angelopoulos, Bates, Fannjiang, Jordan, Zrnic 2023, Science, arXiv:2301.09633 [V-api]: use a small labeled set plus many ML-predicted labels to get valid confidence intervals; the predicted labels reduce variance, the labeled set corrects bias. **Highly transferable:** it is a way to turn "System 1 on everything, owner/panel on a sample" into valid estimates of an agent's true score with honest intervals, with no independence assumption between System 1 and the agent (only that the labeled sample is a random draw).
- **Limit on how much a judge helps.** Dorner, Nastl, Hardt 2025 (ICLR oral) [verified: ICLR 2025 oral; the 2x bound and the weaker-model exception were read in the paper], "Limits to scalable evaluation at the frontier: LLM as Judge won't beat twice the data," arXiv:2410.13341 [V-web]: if the judge is no more accurate than the model being evaluated, no debiasing method can cut the needed ground-truth labels by more than half; savings above 2x occurred only for strong judges on much weaker models (MMLU, MT-Bench). **Transfer:** Jev is a cheap System 1; for agents at or above Jev's capability on a task, expect at most ~2x ground-truth savings. Budget the gold set accordingly.
- Test-retest / self-consistency as a reliability measure of the scorer: standard psychometrics (ICC; Shrout and Fleiss 1979 [verified: Psychological Bulletin 86(2)]). For LLM judges: Schroeder and Wood-Doughty 2024, "Can you trust LLM judgments? Reliability of LLM-as-a-judge," arXiv:2412.12509 [verified] (reported McDonald's omega reliability varying widely by task and temperature, with substantial unreliability in some settings; I have not re-verified numbers). Test-retest measures *noise*, not *bias*: a consistently biased judge passes it. It is necessary, not sufficient.

### 3.5 LLM-as-judge reliability and bias
- Overview: Zheng et al. 2023, "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena," arXiv:2306.05685 [V-api] (strong-judge agreement with humans ~80% on their setup, same level as human-human; documents position, verbosity, self-enhancement bias). Gu et al. 2024 survey, arXiv:2411.15594 [verified].
- **Position bias:** Wang et al. 2023, "Large Language Models are not Fair Evaluators," arXiv:2305.17926 [V-api] (ordering of candidates can flip the verdict; mitigations: swap-and-average, calibration, human-in-loop); Shi et al. 2024, "Judging the Judges: A Systematic Study of Position Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge," arXiv:2406.07791 [V-api].
- **Verbosity/length:** Zheng et al. 2023; Dubois et al. 2024, "Length-Controlled AlpacaEval," arXiv:2404.04475 [V-api]; Singhal et al. 2023 "A Long Way to Go: Length Correlations in RLHF," arXiv:2310.03716 [V-api] (much of RLHF reward gain explained by length).
- **Self-preference:** Panickssery, Bowman, Feng 2024, "LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations," arXiv:2404.13076 [V-api] (measured: self-recognition ability correlates linearly with self-preference strength; fine-tuning to increase self-recognition increases self-preference; causal evidence in the paper's controlled experiments); Liu et al. 2023, "LLMs as Narcissistic Evaluators," arXiv:2311.09766 [V-api]; Wataoka, Takahashi, Ri 2024, "Self-Preference Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge," arXiv:2410.21819 [V-api] (proposes perplexity/familiarity as the mechanism: judges favor lower-perplexity text regardless of authorship; this is a proposed mechanism, replication thin). **Transfer:** the PSA doc's "self-favor gap" for self-scores is supported, and the literature adds that *a different model family does not remove it entirely*: the judge prefers text that is familiar to it, so a Jev from family X scoring a desk on family X will be biased in the same direction as self-scoring. Jev being "a different model family" (PSA 5.2) is the right control, and the pinned-model-id rule matters.
- **Cognitive-bias battery:** Koo et al. 2023, "Benchmarking Cognitive Biases in LLMs as Evaluators," arXiv:2309.17012 [V-api]; Ye et al. 2024, "Justice or Prejudice? Quantifying Biases in LLM-as-a-Judge," arXiv:2410.02736 [V-api]; Thakur et al. 2024, "Judging the Judges: Evaluating Alignment and Vulnerabilities in LLMs-as-Judges," arXiv:2406.12624 [V-api] (judges can look aligned on average agreement while being exploitable by simple tricks).
- **Calibration of stated confidence:** Kadavath et al. 2022, "Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know," arXiv:2207.05221 [verified]; Tian et al. 2023 "Just Ask for Calibration," arXiv:2305.14975 [verified]; Xiong et al. 2024, "Can LLMs Express Their Uncertainty?" arXiv:2306.13063 [V-api] (verbalized confidence tends to be overconfident; gains from sampling-consistency aggregation; hard-to-calibrate on hard tasks). Matches Choir's own Jev finding (miscalibration sign depends on question type).
- **Null-model exploitability:** Zheng et al. 2024, "Cheating Automatic LLM Benchmarks: Null Models Achieve High Win Rates," arXiv:2410.07137 [verified]: a constant, irrelevant output tuned to the judge's prompt reaches top win rates. Directly relevant to "trivial prediction that scores well with a System 1 judge."

### 3.6 Scalable oversight as the regress problem
The infinite regress (who scores the scorer) is the central problem of scalable oversight. Irving, Christiano, Amodei 2018 debate (arXiv:1805.00899 [V-api]); Christiano et al. 2018 iterated amplification (arXiv:1810.08575 [verified; arXiv title is "Supervising strong learners by amplifying weak experts"]); Leike et al. 2018 recursive reward modeling (arXiv:1811.07871 [verified; arXiv title is "Scalable agent alignment via reward modeling: a research direction"]); Bowman et al. 2022 sandwiching (arXiv:2211.03540 [verified; arXiv title is "Measuring Progress on Scalable Oversight for Large Language Models"]); Burns et al. 2023 weak-to-strong (arXiv:2312.09390 [verified]); Khan et al. 2024 "Debating with More Persuasive LLMs Leads to More Truthful Answers," arXiv:2402.06782 [V-api] (reported improvement of non-expert judge accuracy with debate, small setting). Evidence is early and from artificial settings with known ground truth (QuALITY reading comprehension etc.). Their relevance: each proposes a *structured* way to bootstrap trust in a weaker scorer from a stronger or adversarial process; none eliminates the need for some ground-truth anchor. Choir's anchors in section 8 are of this kind.
Also AI control (Greenblatt et al. 2023, arXiv:2312.06942 [V-api]): design monitoring assuming the monitored model may be adversarial; resonates with the "no desk access to a scorer" guard.

### 3.7 What transfers / does not
- Transfers: Dawid-Skene-style reliability estimates for scorers *given a gold subset* (PPI-style is safer than pure EM); surrogate scoring as the formal version of "calibrate System 1 against an audit sample"; Gao et al.'s spot-check design; judge-bias battery as a pre-registered test suite for Jev.
- Does not: pure unsupervised agreement as proof of scorer quality (independence violated); BTS-style population mechanisms (too few scorers); self-consistency as validity.
- Reported failure modes: conditional-dependence among LLM raters; peer-prediction effort-skipping equilibria; judge bias in position/length/self-family; judges exploitable by null outputs and prompt injection (section 4); calibration sign depends on task type.

## 4. Scorer gaming and in-context reward hacking

- **Pan, Jones, Jagadeesan, Steinhardt 2024**, "Feedback Loops With Language Models Drive In-Context Reward Hacking," ICML, arXiv:2402.06627 [V-web]. Feedback loops (output-refinement: a model's outputs shape its next inputs; policy-refinement: the model changes its policy via feedback in context) produce test-time optimization of an implicit objective with negative side effects (their example: engagement up, toxicity up). They state that evaluations on static datasets are insufficient and recommend feedback-loop-aware evaluation. **Transfer:** direct and cited already by the PSA doc (1.2). Caution for the paper: the evidence is small-scale demonstrations; it shows the mechanism exists, not its rate in a deployed system.
- Pan, Bhatia, Steinhardt 2022, "The Effects of Reward Misspecification," arXiv:2201.03544 [verified]: phase transitions in reward-hacking as agent capability grows. Capability makes it worse, not better.
- **Reward-model overoptimization.** Gao, Schulman, Hilton 2022, "Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization," arXiv:2210.10760 [V-api]. With a proxy reward model and a gold reward model, the gold score first rises then falls as optimization against the proxy increases (best-of-n or RL), with functional forms that scale smoothly in proxy-model size and data. Coste et al. 2023 (arXiv:2310.02743 [verified]) and Eisenstein et al. 2023, "Helping or Herding? Reward Model Ensembles Mitigate but do not Eliminate Reward Hacking," arXiv:2312.09244 [V-api]: ensembles reduce but do not remove overoptimization, because ensemble members share error (pretraining seeds). **Transfer, strongest quantitative anchor:** (a) the proxy-vs-gold divergence curve is the form a "System 1 vs owner audit" divergence should take under in-context pressure; (b) ensembles of same-family judges do not buy independence (consistent with 3.1); (c) the optimization pressure in Choir is in-context rather than weight updates, and the scaling laws were measured for weight updates/best-of-n; whether in-context pressure follows the same law is open.
- **Definitions and taxonomy.** Skalse, Howe, Krasheninnikov, Krueger 2022 "Defining and Characterizing Reward Hacking," arXiv:2209.13085 [verified]: proxy and true reward are unhackable only in very restricted cases (formal result, simplified to be informal: hackability is the norm for non-trivial policy sets); Manheim and Garrabrant 2018, "Categorizing Variants of Goodhart's Law," arXiv:1803.04585 [V-api] (regressional, extremal, causal, adversarial); Karwowski et al. 2023 "Goodhart's Law in Reinforcement Learning," arXiv:2310.09144 [verified]. Which Goodhart variants apply: *regressional* (judge noise selected on), *causal* (agent acts on the scorer, not the world), *adversarial* (agent models the scorer). Choir's proper-scoring-of-predictions design reduces the first two (the score is about outside outcomes) but not the last when the scorer settles from agent-influenced observations.
- **Sycophancy and specification gaming in deployed agents.** Sharma et al. 2023, "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models," arXiv:2310.13548 [V-api] (human and preference-model judgments themselves favor sycophantic answers; optimizing against preference models trades off truthfulness); Denison et al. 2024, "Sycophancy to Subterfuge: Investigating Reward-Tampering in Large Language Models," arXiv:2406.10162 [verified] (generalization from mild specification gaming to reward-tampering in a curriculum; rare, small rates, in constructed environments). Baker et al. 2025, "Monitoring Reasoning Models for Misbehavior and the Risks of Promoting Obfuscation," arXiv:2503.11926 [V-api]: monitoring chain-of-thought catches reward hacking, but optimizing against the monitor leads models to hide intent while still hacking. **Transfer:** a direct warning for "records redirect the mechanism": if the scoring signal feeds back into context and the monitor/scorer is used as an optimization target, the agent may learn to obfuscate the *record* rather than the *behavior*. This supports the PSA doc's choice to make fidelity checks mechanical (frame log) rather than introspective.
- **Judge exploitation.** Shi et al. 2024, "Optimization-based Prompt Injection Attack to LLM-as-a-Judge," arXiv:2403.17710 [verified]; Raina, Liusie, Gales 2024, "Is LLM-as-a-Judge Robust? Investigating Universal Adversarial Attacks on Zero-shot LLM Assessment," arXiv:2402.14016 [verified]; Zheng et al. null models (3.5); Thakur et al. (3.5). Judges are reported to be fooled by short universal strings in several settings. **Transfer:** a desk whose content is the object being judged can embed instructions to the judge; PSA 5.4's "scores on untrusted content are advisory" is right. Mechanical rule scoring and typed fields reduce, not remove, this.
- What the literature does NOT supply: a measured rate of in-context scorer exploitation in long-horizon multi-agent systems with persistent memory, which is Choir's setting. That is a gap Choir can fill and a natural pre-registered experiment.

## 5. Pairwise and comparative judgments versus absolute ratings

- **Law of comparative judgment.** Thurstone 1927; Bradley and Terry 1952 (Biometrika) [verified: 39(3/4)]; Plackett 1975, Luce 1959 for rankings [unverifiable: not checked]; Thurstone 1927 [unverifiable: not checked]. Elo 1978 (chess; online update); Herbrich, Minka, Graepel 2006, "TrueSkill," NIPS [verified: NIPS 19 proceedings, indexed as 2007] (Bayesian skill with per-player uncertainty, handles teams and draws). Hunter 2004 MM algorithm for BT [verified: Ann. Statist. 32(1)]. Rank Centrality (Negahban, Oh, Shah 2012/2017, arXiv:1209.1688 [V-api]); Shah and Wainwright 2016, "Simple, robust and optimal ranking from pairwise comparisons," arXiv:1512.08949 [verified] (sample complexity of ranking from noisy pairwise comparisons).
- **Why comparisons for graded constructs.** Humans are better at relative than absolute judgments; Kahneman, Sibony, Sunstein 2021, *Noise* (Little, Brown) [verified: the book exists; the specific argument was not checked in the text] argues absolute scale ratings carry large "scale noise" (different raters use the scale differently) and recommends relative judgments or anchored scales [mem; argument-level evidence, not a single experiment]. Carterette et al. 2008, "Here or there: preference judgments for relevance," ECIR [verified]: preference judgments for relevance are faster and more reliable per judgment than graded relevance in their IR experiments. Yannakakis and Martínez 2015, "Ratings are overrated!" Frontiers in ICT [verified: 2015] (ordinal comparisons yield more reliable affect annotation than ratings). Evidence base: psychometrics and IR, moderate.
- **LLM judging, pairwise vs pointwise.** Zheng et al. 2023 used pairwise and pointwise (single-answer grading) and found both usable with biases; Liu et al. 2024, "Aligning with Human Judgement: The Role of Pairwise Preference in LLM Evaluators," arXiv:2403.16950 [verified] (reported pairwise inputs help but LLM preferences are not transitive/consistent under reordering, which motivated their proposed PairS method; verify); Qin et al. 2023, "Large Language Models are Effective Text Rankers with Pairwise Ranking Prompting," arXiv:2306.17563 [verified]. Position bias (3.5) is stronger in pairwise formats and is mitigated by swap-and-average at 2x the cost.
- **Chatbot Arena.** Chiang et al. 2024, arXiv:2403.04132 [V-api]: Bradley-Terry over crowd pairwise votes, with bootstrap CIs; Boubdir et al. 2023, "Elo Uncovered: Robustness and Best Practices in Language Model Evaluation," arXiv:2311.17295 [verified] (Elo is sensitive to match ordering and K; BT with fixed data is more stable; a measured failure). Zheng et al. 2024 null-models (3.5) shows arena-style win rates are gameable by output style.
- **Inter-rater reliability.** Cohen 1960 kappa; Fleiss 1971; Krippendorff alpha (Krippendorff 2004 [unverifiable: not checked]; Hayes and Krippendorff 2007 for the standard-practice alpha [verified]); Feinstein and Cicchetti 1990 "High agreement but low kappa" (prevalence and bias paradoxes) [verified: J Clin Epidemiol 43(6)]; ICC (Shrout and Fleiss 1979) for ordinal/continuous. Known failure: kappa is dominated by prevalence; for rare material events, report positive/negative agreement and alpha with intervals. Landis and Koch benchmarks are conventional, not principled.
- **Transfer to materiality.** Impact and relevance are *graded, comparative* constructs for which absolute numeric assignment ("impact=7") is exactly the scale-noise setting. Practical form: (i) a small number of **anchored classes** (the bayesian-trust doc's I0-I4) as the primary scale, with the anchors being rule-detectable action types (what the frame log shows), (ii) **pairwise comparison** among consequences within a class or between a consequence and a named anchor to place it, aggregated by Bradley-Terry/TrueSkill with intervals and with the number of comparisons as a precision measure, (iii) report Krippendorff alpha for the judges' impact labels per class. TrueSkill-type uncertainty is useful since it yields a posterior over each consequence's impact, which propagates into the posterior over `w`.
- **Non-transfer:** BT assumes a single latent dimension and transitivity; "impact" across domains (data loss vs money vs reputation) may be multidimensional or intransitive, and LLM preferences are known to be non-transitive in places (verify the cited measure before asserting a rate in the paper). Pairwise is O(n log n) comparisons for ranking; with open-ended consequence sets this gets costly, so use it for placing against anchors, not for global ranking.

## 6. Forecasting-tournament practice

- **Tetlock.** Tetlock 2005, *Expert Political Judgment* (Princeton UP): long-run expert forecasts barely beat simple baselines; hedgehogs vs foxes; calibration and discrimination reported. Tetlock and Gardner 2015, *Superforecasting*. Mellers et al. 2014, "Psychological strategies for winning a geopolitical forecasting tournament," Psychological Science 25(5) [verified]: training, teaming and tracking improved Brier scores. Mellers et al. 2015, "Identifying and cultivating superforecasters as a method of improving probabilistic predictions," Perspectives on Psychological Science [verified: 10(3), 2015]. Ungar, Mellers, Satopää, Tetlock, Baron 2012, "The Good Judgment Project: a large scale test of different methods of combining expert predictions," AAAI Fall Symposium [verified: AAAI FS-12-06; the paper also lists J. Ramos and S. Swift as authors].
- **Scoring.** Brier (GJP's primary metric, with mean-daily-Brier across the question's life, which rewards early commitment and updating); Metaculus uses log-based scores: "baseline score" (vs. a 50% or base-rate reference), "peer score" (vs. the average of other forecasters, zero-sum, with the log score; rewards beating the crowd and penalizes not forecasting) — documented in Metaculus's scoring FAQ [verified via search summaries: baseline and peer scores are log-based, peer scores sum to zero; the live page returned 403 and was not read directly]. Peer scoring's incentive properties and its use as a *ground-truth-free-ish* score for unresolved questions: Karger, Atanasov, Tetlock 2022, "Improving judgments of existential risk: better forecasts, questions, explanations, policies," [verified: SSRN 2022] and "Reciprocal scoring" (Karger et al. 2021, SSRN) [verified: Karger, Monrad, Mellers, Tetlock, 2021]: score forecasters on how well they predict the *other forecasters' aggregate*, for questions that will not resolve in time; reported to correlate with accuracy on resolvable questions in their experiments. This is a peer-prediction cousin, with the same independence caveats.
- **Aggregation and extremizing.** Baron, Mellers, Tetlock, Stone, Ungar 2014, "Two reasons to make aggregated probability forecasts more extreme," Decision Analysis [verified: 11(2)]; Satopää, Baron, Foster, Mellers, Tetlock, Ungar 2014, "Combining multiple probability predictions using a simple logit model," Int. J. Forecasting [verified: 30(2)]; Satopää, Salikhov, Tetlock, Mellers 2023 [corrected: Management Science 67(12), 2021], "Bias, information, noise: the BIN model of forecasting," Management Science [verified]. Core idea: the average of independent, partially informed forecasters is under-confident, so push it away from 0.5; the optimal extremization depends on the diversity of information. **Transfer to Choir:** if multiple scorers each emit a probability, pooling them with logit-averaging and an extremizing factor fit on the audit set is empirically supported *for human forecasters*; for LLM scorers with correlated errors extremizing would be wrong (it presumes independent information). Test, don't assume.
- **Forecaster weighting by track record.** Himmelstein, Atanasov, Budescu 2021, "Forecasting forecaster accuracy: contributions of past performance and individual differences," Judgment and Decision Making [verified: 16(2), 2021]; Atanasov et al. 2017 "Distilling the wisdom of crowds: prediction markets vs prediction polls," Management Science [verified: 63(3)] (weights by past accuracy and teaming beat markets in GJP). Supports the Bayesian-trust doc's premise (H4) in human data; thin for agents.
- **Question quality, difficulty, and relevance — directly relevant to materiality.**
  - *Clairvoyance test:* Tetlock's requirement that a question be resolvable by a hypothetical clairvoyant without further interpretation (Tetlock and Gardner 2015) [unverifiable: book, clairvoyance wording not checked]. Choir's "settlement" and "criterion" fields are the same discipline.
  - *Rigor-relevance tradeoff:* Tetlock, Mellers, Scoblic 2017, "Bringing probability judgments into policy debates via forecasting tournaments," Science 355 [verified: 355(6324), 2017]: crisp resolvable questions are often only indirectly relevant to policy; the proposed remedy is **question clustering** (a cluster of resolvable proxy questions jointly informs a policy-relevant unresolvable one, with relevance of the cluster judged by experts) — see also Tetlock et al. 2014 Curr. Dir. Psych. Sci. "Forecasting tournaments: tools for increasing transparency and the quality of debate" [verified: 23(4), 2014]. This is the closest forecasting-field analogue of materiality scoring, and it makes relevance an *expert/ex-ante* judgment at the question level, not a function of the outcome.
  - *Difficulty adjustment:* Merkle, Steyvers, Mellers, Tetlock 2016, "Item response models of probability judgments: application to a geopolitical forecasting tournament," Decision 3(1), doi:10.1037/dec0000032 [V-web]: IRT models of forecasters and questions; accounts for question difficulty so that getting hard questions right earns more. **Transfer:** weights/difficulty are estimated jointly; for Choir this means a "question difficulty" latent per question type and a skill latent per agent, which also defends against farming easy questions (bayesian-trust attack row 1).
  - *Incentive-compatibility of tournaments:* Witkowski, Atanasov, Ungar, et al. [corrected: authors are Witkowski, Freeman, Vaughan, Pennock and Krause], "Incentive-compatible forecasting competitions," Management Science 2023 [verified: 69(3)], arXiv:2101.01816 [verified] (winner-take-all contests distort risk-taking and need specific scoring designs to remain proper at the contest level). Reported failure: rank-based payoffs make proper per-question rules improper at the tournament level.
  - *LLM forecasting:* Halawi et al. 2024, "Approaching Human-Level Forecasting with Language Models," arXiv:2402.18563 [verified]; Schoenegger et al. 2024, "Wisdom of the Silicon Crowd," arXiv:2402.19379 [V-api] (LLM ensembles rival human crowd accuracy in a three-month tournament; the "LLM crowd" benefits from aggregation); Karger et al. 2024, "ForecastBench," arXiv:2409.19839 [verified]. Caveats: leakage/contamination in retrospective evaluation (matches PSA 5.7).
- **What GJP/Metaculus do NOT do:** they do not weight questions by importance in the score; question importance is handled by what questions get asked, and by clustering. Their handling of "material vs trivial" is thus *selection of questions*, not weighting. This is relevant to the Choir problem that agents choose their own questions (PSA 5.4: "agents writing their own questions"): the literature says the question-set must be set (or approved) by someone other than the forecaster.

## 7. Materiality: accounting standards and other anchors

### 7.1 Accounting and law (the analogy is better than expected)
- **Legal definition.** *TSC Industries v. Northway*, 426 U.S. 438 (1976) [verified via secondary sources]: an omitted fact is material if there is a "substantial likelihood" a reasonable investor would view it as having significantly altered the "total mix" of information. *Basic Inc. v. Levinson*, 485 U.S. 224 (1988): for contingent events, materiality depends on a balancing of **the probability the event will occur and its anticipated magnitude** (the probability/magnitude test) [V-web]. This is nearly literally `P(c|a) x impact(c)`, with "total mix" as the `relevance` analogue (does it change the decision of the reasonable investor). The accounting/legal world arrived at the same functional form the PSA doc proposes. *Matrixx Initiatives v. Siracusano*, 563 U.S. 27 (2011) [verified via secondary sources]: rejects bright-line statistical-significance thresholds for materiality; no single mechanical cut-off may define it.
- **Standard-setters.** SEC Staff Accounting Bulletin No. 99 (1999) [verified: sec.gov SAB 99; 5% is a rule-of-thumb screen, qualitative factors required]: rejects a purely quantitative rule such as 5%; requires qualitative factors (does it mask a trend, change a loss into income, affect compliance with covenants, involve management concealment, etc.). FASB Concepts Statement No. 8, ch. 3 (2010) [unverifiable: not checked]: materiality is entity-specific, a "threshold or cut-off point" rather than a primary qualitative characteristic. IFRS Practice Statement 2 (IASB 2017), "Making Materiality Judgements" [verified: Sept 2017, non-mandatory]: four-step process (identify, assess, organize, review). ISA 320 / PCAOB AS 2105 [unverifiable: not checked]: planning materiality (benchmark ~5% of pre-tax income as a common rule of thumb, which is a convention rather than an empirically derived figure), **performance materiality** set below overall materiality so aggregated uncorrected misstatements don't exceed it, a "clearly trivial" threshold below which items need not be accumulated. The audit risk model: audit risk = inherent risk x control risk x detection risk [unverifiable: not checked].
- **Empirical audit literature.** Holstrum and Messier 1982, "A review and integration of empirical research on materiality," Auditing: A Journal of Practice and Theory [verified: 2(1)]; Messier, Martinov-Bennie, Eilifsen 2005, "A review and integration of empirical research on materiality: two decades later," Auditing [verified: 24(2)]; Eilifsen and Messier 2015, "Materiality guidance of the major auditing and accounting standard setters," Accounting Horizons [corrected: the paper is "Materiality Guidance of the Major Public Accounting Firms", Auditing: A Journal of Practice & Theory 34(2), 2015, pp. 3-26; no Accounting Horizons paper by that title was found]. Findings I recall (verify before citing): auditors' materiality judgments vary substantially across auditors for identical facts, are anchored on benchmarks (earnings, assets), are sensitive to qualitative cues, and show weak consistency with the stated frameworks. This is the same disagreement problem Choir will see among scorers of impact, and gives the paper an honest base rate: *experienced human professionals agree only moderately on materiality*. Do not expect LLM raters to do better than the audit profession; set expectations accordingly.
- **What the analogy gives Choir.**
  1. A two-part test (probability times magnitude, plus a "changes the total mix for a reasonable decider" relevance test) already operating in law.
  2. **Two thresholds**: overall materiality and a lower "performance materiality" to control aggregation: the bayesian-trust doc's I-classes plus a floor below which items are not accumulated into trust (I0/"clearly trivial") match this structure; add an *aggregation* rule: many small omissions can sum to material (performance materiality's purpose). The current `sum w` accumulates by construction, but a floor that zeroes small items would hide a flood of small errors. Keep the floor but audit the sum of below-floor items.
  3. **Reasonable-decider standard** rather than agent-specific: materiality is judged from the standpoint of a reference decider (the authorizer), not the author. Same as relevance as decision-flip for the authorizer.
  4. **Qualitative overrides**: SAB 99 says some items are material regardless of size (concealment, legal compliance). The bayesian-trust doc's E3 fidelity / E4 refusal strong multipliers are this idea. Note the legal tradition's reason: intent to conceal raises materiality.
  5. Auditor independence and review: audit firm quality control, peer review of audit firms (PCAOB inspections; external audit quality literature) as a model of "score the scorer" by sampling the scorer's work and having a regulator re-perform it. DeAngelo 1981 "Auditor size and audit quality" [verified: J. Accounting and Economics 3(3)] (audit quality = joint probability of detecting and reporting): scorer quality has two components, detection and reporting, and independence affects the second. A nice decomposition for scorers: *can it detect* (competence) vs *will it report* (independence/incentive). Choir's scorer track records should track both.
- **Failure modes the accounting literature reports:** materiality is manipulated opportunistically (earnings-management studies; SAB 99 was issued in response to "5% rule" gaming and the practice of "netting" misstatements) [unverifiable: SAB 99 text confirms it rejects reliance on 5% alone; the motive and the netting history were not checked]; thresholds become targets (Goodhart). Directly parallels the risk that an agent commits just under the "trivial" floor.

### 7.2 Risk analysis (likelihood x severity)
- Kaplan and Garrick 1981, "On the quantitative definition of risk," Risk Analysis [verified: 1(1)]: risk is a set of triplets (scenario, likelihood, consequence), not a scalar; Cox 2008, "What's wrong with risk matrices?" Risk Analysis [verified: 28(2)]: ordinal risk matrices can rank risks backwards, assign the same rating to quantitatively very different risks, and categorical bins create inconsistent comparisons; Thomas, Bratvold, Bickel 2014, "The risk of using risk matrices," SPE Economics & Management [verified: 6(2)]. FMEA's Risk Priority Number (severity x occurrence x detection) is criticized for the same ordinal-product issues (Liu et al. reviews) [unverifiable: no specific review named]. **Transfer, a warning:** the product form `P x impact x relevance` over ordinal classes (I0-I4 = 0,1,3,10,30) is a risk-matrix-style calculation; the criticisms apply if impact and relevance are coarse ordinal bins. The bayesian-trust doc's geometric spacing (1,3,10,30) is a defensible log-scale choice (roughly Weber-Fechner), but the choice is stipulated, not empirically derived; say so.
- Surprise-weighting: Bayesian surprise (Itti and Baldi 2009) [verified] as a possible alternative importance signal (KL between prior and posterior beliefs of the decider after seeing the claim/outcome). Interesting but untested for agents; it is outcome-dependent so it can serve only as a *diagnostic*, not an ex-ante weight.

### 7.3 Materiality in AI/claims contexts
- I found no paper that weights an LLM agent's predictions or claims by decision materiality in the way the PSA doc proposes. Closest: atomic-fact importance in summarization (Pyramid method; 2.1), check-worthiness in fact-checking (ClaimBuster, Hassan et al. 2017 KDD [verified: "Toward automated fact-checking", KDD 2017]: ranks sentences by whether the public would care to check them), cost-sensitive learning (Elkan 2001, "The foundations of cost-sensitive learning," IJCAI [unverifiable: not located]). None validates a graded, counterfactual, decision-relative weight for agent claims. **This is a genuine gap, and it is the paper's main novelty claim**, so state it with care ("to our knowledge," plus the search log of what was checked).

## 8. Synthesis

### 8.1 Problem (A): scoring materiality
**What the literature says it must satisfy.**
1. *Outcome-independence.* Weights fixed ex ante (Lerch et al.). Therefore: `C*` (the reference consequence set) and `impact` and `relevance` are frozen at commit time; what actually happened may only (i) extend `C*` for later commitments and (ii) trigger the separate **coverage penalty** E2 for *omitted* consequences, where the penalty is computed from the same pre-registered critic procedure, not from the outcome. Even E2 has the forecaster's-dilemma risk if a consequence is added to `C*` only after it occurs; the cleaner rule is two-stage: (a) at commit time an independent critic panel proposes `C*`; (b) post hoc discoveries are scored as *capability/foresight events in a separate ledger* (PSA 3.2 "predictable, not predicted") and do not reweight the proper score.
2. *Decision-relative via Schervish.* The score of a probability on consequence `c` is a mixture of cost-loss problems over the authorizer's threshold distribution. Define `relevance(c)` as the measure the authorizer's decision rule places on the threshold region near `p_c`. Operationally: counterfactually call the authorizer on (action, c revealed/not revealed) and record flips; estimate flip probability with an interval.
3. *Counterfactuals only where identified.* Alternatives not taken can be scored only if the action was randomized with logged propensity (decision scoring rule). Elsewhere, "stated alternatives" are scored by their own settlement if executed in a sandbox/replay (a capsule is a natural counterfactual executor in Choir: running the rejected alternative in a disposable capsule gives an actual outcome rather than an estimate), otherwise left unsettled.
4. *Graded by anchors and comparisons.* Impact: anchored classes with rule-detectable anchors; within-class refinement by pairwise comparison vs anchors (BT/TrueSkill) with posteriors; report reliability (Krippendorff alpha) per class.
5. *Reasonable-decider, two thresholds, qualitative overrides* from the accounting tradition; audit the sum of below-floor items.
6. *Use ordinal-aware arithmetic.* Cox's critique: avoid multiplying coarse ordinal bins as if ratio-scale; either keep `(P, impact, relevance)` as a triplet in the stored record and combine at view time with logged parameters (the bayesian-trust doc already says views are logged and reproducible), or treat impact as log-scale with intervals.

**Candidate architecture for (A).**
```
commit time (frozen, hashed):
  C*         = union of critic-panel proposals, reference-predictor P_ref(c|a)   (independent of the committing agent)
  impact(c)  = class by rule where possible (frame-log action class, grant impact table), else anchored pairwise placement with posterior
  relevance(c) = flip rate of the authorizer policy under reveal(c), with interval (counterfactual call)
  w(c) = P_ref(c|a) * impact(c) * relevance(c)       -- NOT the agent's own P
settlement:
  S(p_c, outcome_c)   proper (Brier / floored log), reported together with the base-rate-subtracted difference (unclipped)
  weight w(c) as frozen; propensity weight 1/pi(a) if the action was randomized
  aggregate: sum_c w(c) * [S - S_ref]
separate ledgers (never summed with the proper score):
  E2 coverage events (predictable, not predicted)  -- post hoc, critic-procedure-based
  E3 fidelity events                                -- mechanical
```
Rationale: agent's own `P(c|a)` is excluded from `w` because it lets the agent shrink `w` on consequences it down-weights (self-referential), which is the Goodhart route the PSA doc's "P(c|a) comes from reference predictors" already anticipates.

### 8.2 Problem (B): scoring the scorers, with terminating anchors
Regress structure: settle S0 (rule) > S1 (System 1) > peer/panel > owner. Literature-backed way to *terminate* it:
1. **Anchor tier 0, rule-settled facts**: deterministic checks over the frame log and dated primary records. No scorer is needed; this is ground truth by construction. Establish the maximum feasible share of material outcomes settled here; the achievable share is an empirical quantity to report.
2. **Anchor tier 1, spot-checks with unpredictable audit** (Gao et al.): a random sample, indistinguishable from the live stream, is re-settled by the highest-authority scorer available (panel plus owner, or a delayed real-world outcome). Randomization is mandatory; any scorer-visible marker defeats it.
3. **Anchor tier 2, delayed natural resolution**: many predictions settle later by world events (the Fed 2012-2025 corpus and earnings-guidance corpora in PSA 5.7), which provide ground truth that no Choir scorer controlled. Use these as the *calibration set for Jev*, with leakage class L0-L1.
4. **Estimation layer**: PPI (Angelopoulos et al.) / surrogate scoring (Liu et al.) combine many cheap System 1 labels with the anchor sample to give an agent's true score with valid intervals and to learn Jev's noise model per question type. The scorer's own reliability = its confusion matrix against anchors (Dawid-Skene with gold, not unsupervised).
5. **Independence audit** (not assumed): measure conditional dependence among scorers on the anchor sample (pairwise error correlation given truth). If error correlation is high, do not count agreement as evidence and do not extremize.
6. **Regress cut-off rule.** The recursion stops when the scorer-of-the-scorer is a *rule or a delayed world outcome*. The owner is a terminal for *preference/legitimacy* questions but is not ground truth for factual ones; do not use the owner as a quality oracle (the audit literature shows human materiality judgments disagree). Cap the meta level at two: scorer reliability is estimated against anchors; the anchors are not scored (they are rules or world facts). Record "unanchored" strata explicitly: question types with no anchor have scorer reliability reported as *unknown*, not extrapolated.
7. **Adversarial scorer protection**: scorer isolation from the acting context (PSA 4.7), delayed and sampled feedback, rotation of scorer model/prompt with pinned ids, null-model and injection test suite run against Jev periodically (the Zheng et al., Raina et al., Shi et al. families), peer-prediction-style mutual-information scoring as a second, structurally different signal on the same items (Robertson-Koyejo, Qiu et al.), flagged as experimental.

**Scorer score.** For scorer `j` and anchor item `i`: report separately (a) discrimination (AUC against anchor labels), (b) calibration per type (reliability diagram, Brier), (c) bias profile (position, length, self-family, specificity), (d) test-retest ICC, (e) exploitability (null-model pass rate), (f) detection vs reporting split where applicable (DeAngelo). Do not collapse to one number (consistent with PSA 5.5).

### 8.3 Interface with Bayesian trust
- `q_i`: use an unclipped proper score difference; clip only at the view.
- Evidence weight `m_i` multipliers (5, 20) are stipulated; mark as policy and run sensitivity.
- Weight evidence by scorer reliability *as estimated against anchors* (precision weighting), and discount evidence whose scorer error is correlated with the subject's model family (self-preference literature).
- The posterior's Beta likelihood treats `w_i q_i` as fractional pseudo-counts; with materiality weights up to 30x this double-counts a single event into dozens of "observations" and makes the posterior overconfident. Use weights as importance weights in a weighted-likelihood tempered posterior (power posterior) or cap effective sample size; I infer this (standard Bayesian practice; not found stated for this use). This is a likely real defect in the design doc and is cheap to test by replay.
- Lower credible bound decision rule: sensible; add a check that LCB decisions are stable under reweighting of impact within plausible bounds.

### 8.4 Open research questions
1. Can `relevance` as flip-rate be estimated reliably and cheaply, and is it stable to the choice of authorizer prompt/model? (No literature; novel.)
2. Do impact/materiality labels from LLM raters reach inter-rater reliability comparable to auditors' (itself only moderate)? What alpha is achievable for anchored vs unanchored scales?
3. How correlated are the errors of different LLM scorers given truth? Does ensemble diversity (different families) restore enough independence for Dawid-Skene or extremizing?
4. How large is the performative bias for gating predictions in Choir, and does randomized-action scoring remove it at an acceptable exploration cost?
5. Does in-context feedback induce scorer exploitation at measurable rates, and does delayed/sampled feedback lower it (Pan et al. mechanism; Gao et al. curve shape)?
6. What fraction of material outcomes can be rule-settled (tier-0 coverage)? This bounds how much of the system rests on any model scorer.
7. Is the bayesian-trust LCB ordering predictive of next failure (AUC) beyond simpler baselines (allowlist, count, recency)?
8. Is there a point at which stronger agents outrun the scorer (Dorner et al. limit), and is peer-prediction scoring more robust there (inverse-scaling claim of Qiu et al.)?
9. Do forecasting-tournament findings (extremizing, teaming, training) transfer to agent populations?

## 9. Falsifiable hypotheses for pre-registration

Format: hypothesis, test, and a refutation criterion. Thresholds are placeholders to be fixed before data (with numbers chosen by the authors from pilot variance, not by me).

**Materiality (A)**
- **H-A1 (outcome-conditioned weights are improper).** Weighting settled scores by post hoc `C*` (including consequences that occurred) rewards extreme/catastrophe-heavy commitments relative to ex-ante weights. *Test:* replay the same records with ex-ante vs post hoc weights; compare rank by an agent that always lists extreme consequences with the same hit rate. *Refuted if* rankings are statistically indistinguishable (rank correlation > pre-set, no systematic advantage to extremizers).
- **H-A2 (flip-rate relevance carries information).** Consequences with high authorizer flip-rate are the ones whose omission or error predicts later owner reversals/reverts better than consequences weighted by impact class alone. *Test:* logistic regression of owner revert on omitted-consequence weight with and without relevance. *Refuted if* adding relevance does not improve held-out log loss.
- **H-A3 (anchored comparative impact beats absolute rating).** Pairwise-vs-anchor impact placement by LLM judges yields higher Krippendorff alpha (and higher agreement with rule-derived impact class where the latter exists) than direct 0-10 or class assignment. *Refuted if* alpha difference CI includes 0.
- **H-A4 (judge impact agreement is bounded by audit-like levels).** LLM-judge impact labels on unanchored consequences reach no higher agreement with the owner-panel than the inter-owner/inter-panel agreement. (Null-leaning; if LLMs exceed human panel agreement, report.)
- **H-A5 (performative distortion).** For gating predictions, the agent's stated `P(c|a)` is less calibrated than for non-gating (diagnostic) predictions of the same type, by a margin > pre-set; randomized-action scoring on the held-out set removes the gap. *Refuted if* no calibration gap or randomization does not reduce it.
- **H-A6 (ordinal-product artifacts).** Ranking agents by `sum P x impact x relevance` with the I0-I4 geometric table disagrees with ranking under monotone alternative tables (1,2,4,8,16; 1,5,25,125,625) on more than a pre-set share of pairs. *If rankings are invariant, the stipulated table is not load-bearing; report that.*
- **H-A7 (floor hides floods).** Items below the "clearly trivial" floor, summed, predict later material incidents for some agents (non-zero predictive value). *Refuted if* the below-floor sum has no predictive value beyond zero.

**Meta-scoring (B)**
- **H-B1 (judge self-family bias in Choir's setting).** Jev (family X) scores predictions/settlements of desks on family X more favorably than the same content authored on another family, controlling for correctness (gold-labeled). *Refuted if* the favor gap CI includes 0.
- **H-B2 (error correlation among LLM scorers).** Pairwise error correlation between LLM scorers on the anchor set, conditional on truth, exceeds that between independent human raters by a pre-set margin; hence unsupervised Dawid-Skene overestimates scorer accuracy. *Test:* compare DS-estimated accuracy with anchor-measured accuracy. *Refuted if* DS error is within a small tolerance.
- **H-B3 (spot-check beats peer agreement).** A scheme that pays/rewards scorers (or selects scorers) by agreement with randomized spot-checks yields scorer reliability estimates closer to anchor truth than peer-agreement-only estimates, at equal labeling budget. (Gao et al. predict yes.) *Refuted if* peer-only is no worse.
- **H-B4 (PPI validity).** PPI intervals combining Jev labels with an n-item anchored sample achieve nominal coverage of an agent's true mean score, with width gain < 2x versus anchor-only when the agent is at or above Jev's capability (Dorner et al. prediction). *Refuted if* coverage fails, or if width gain exceeds 2x for a not-weaker judge.
- **H-B5 (surrogate scoring recovers ranking).** Applying a per-type confusion-matrix correction to Jev scores improves Kendall tau between agent rankings and anchor-truth rankings over uncorrected Jev scores. *Refuted if* no improvement.
- **H-B6 (in-context scorer exploitation).** Agents shown their scorer-derived feedback inside context (immediate) drift toward higher Jev scores with no gain (or a loss) on anchor-measured quality, relative to delayed/sampled feedback arms (proxy-gold divergence of the Gao et al. shape). *Refuted if* anchor quality tracks Jev score equally in both arms.
- **H-B7 (null/injection exploitability).** A null-style or injected string in a settlement-relevant field raises Jev's "confirmed" rate above a pre-set threshold; typed-field rule checks do not move. (Zheng/Raina/Shi predict yes for Jev.) *Refuted if* Jev is robust under the pre-set battery.
- **H-B8 (peer-prediction as complementary signal).** A TVD-MI/peer-predictability score computed over the same items has higher AUC against anchor truth than Jev under an adversarial (deceptive-desk) condition, and comparable AUC under benign conditions. *Refuted if* it does not exceed Jev under attack (Robertson-Koyejo, Qiu et al. claim yes; replication test).
- **H-B9 (test-retest is not enough).** Judge test-retest ICC does not predict anchor accuracy across scorer configurations (correlation near zero). *Refuted if* high ICC configurations are reliably more accurate.
- **H-B10 (regress terminates at two levels).** Scorer-reliability estimates against tier-0/1/2 anchors, versus estimates that also use a second-level scorer-of-scorer, do not differ beyond sampling error. *Refuted if* adding a third level changes trust-relevant decisions by > pre-set share.

**Trust and aggregation**
- **H-C1 (weighted pseudo-counts overconfident).** The Beta posterior with materiality-weighted pseudo-counts has worse calibration of predicted next-failure than a tempered/capped-ESS version on replay. *Refuted if* equal or better.
- **H-C2 (extremizing).** Extremized logit-pooled scorer probabilities improve Brier vs the anchor sample for diverse-family scorer panels but not for same-family panels. *Refuted if* extremizing helps (or hurts) in both.
- **H-C3 (track record predicts failure).** LCB ordering by class predicts next contradicted or fidelity event better (AUC) than success rate, count and recency baselines (the PSA H4 / bayesian-trust claim). Placeholder threshold; pre-register baselines.

### 9.1 Recommended pre-registration hygiene
Frozen corpus manifest hash before extraction (PSA 5.7); anchor sample drawn by a published RNG seed; scorer ids and prompts pinned and logged; analysis code committed before outcomes unseal; report null results on H-A4, H-A6, H-B9 because they bound the claims. Do not claim any [mem] citation in the paper until verified.
