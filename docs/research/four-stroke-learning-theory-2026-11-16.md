# Call it, check it, learn it, earn it: a four-stroke theory of learning, confidence and trust

*Draft prepared with an AI research assistant*

## Abstract

This paper states a four-phase theory of learning and tests it against the published record. A learner makes a forecast: a claim in advance, with a stated confidence and a test. An outcome arrives that the learner cannot author. The learner writes a lesson, a general statement about the task that changes the next forecast. Trust is then extended or withdrawn ahead of the next outcome, by others and by the learner. The strong claim is that all four are necessary and that removing any one stalls learning or turns it into something else. The evidence is uneven. Error-driven learning, the benefit of committing before feedback, hindsight distortion and the value of scored forecasting are well supported. The claim that every kind of learning needs all four phases is not: retrieval practice strengthens memory without feedback, and statistical learning needs no stated forecast. The social half, in which status and trust are a group's running score of a person's commitments and confidence is the same score kept from inside, is partly supported and partly contradicted, since groups often reward displayed confidence over calibration. We narrow the claim accordingly and say what would falsify it.

## 1. The theory

Most accounts of learning name one ingredient, such as practice, feedback, motivation or memory, and treat it as the whole. The theory examined here says that learning which makes progress, as opposed to learning which only repeats, is a cycle of four phases in a fixed order, and that each phase does work the others cannot do. Following the engineering image in which the theory was first put, we call the phases strokes. The short form is: call it, check it, learn it, earn it.

The first stroke is the forecast. Before acting, the learner says what they expect to happen, how sure they are, and what observation would settle it. A forecast is more than an intention to try. It is a claim that can turn out wrong, made at a time when the learner does not yet know whether it will.

The second stroke is the outcome. Something other than the learner says what happened. The theory attaches a rule to this stroke: you do not mark your own work. The rule has two parts. The one who made the forecast does not settle it, and the one whose act is being judged cannot write the result. An outcome the actor can write is not evidence. A further distinction follows, because outcomes differ in how well they teach. A good one is prompt, specific and cannot be flattered. A listener who walks away, a bridge that stands, a market that clears and a test that fails are hard to flatter. A judge with a rubric the learner has read is easier.

The third stroke is the lesson. When the outcome meets the forecast, the learner writes down the most general thing learned, in their own words, in a place where their next attempt will read it. The theory is particular about what counts. A lesson is about the task and not the self: "estimates ran short when the job had hidden dependencies, four times in five" teaches, where "you are overconfident" invites a uniform shift that loses discrimination. A lesson cites the record that justified it. It is itself a forecast, that errors of this kind will now fall, and so it can be scored like anything else. Most outcomes confirm an existing lesson, and new lessons come from surprises.

The fourth stroke is trust. Trust is what the learner is given ahead of the next outcome: more room, larger stakes, less checking before the act, or the reverse. Trust does not replace checking, since everything still gets an outcome. It decides whether the check must come before the act or may come after it, and the stakes set how much trust that takes. The theory then makes its least familiar move. Trust is also turned inward. A learner's confidence is said to be earned the same way as others' trust in them, by a record of having called it and been right. Trust is the flywheel: it carries what was earned into the next cycle, and because a lesson is weighed according to its source, it joins the fourth stroke back to the first.

A second rule governs the whole cycle: nothing is held about you that you cannot read. The learner gets the marked paper back. A test may stay unseen until it is marked, but nothing stays hidden for good. Readable does not mean writable. Independence, and not secrecy, is the defence against gaming.

A scored forecast yields two quantities with two different readers. Calibration asks whether the stated confidence was honest. Its reader is the learner, who uses it for the next forecast. Reliability asks whether the result met the standard the other party set. Its reader is whoever depends on the learner. The two come apart. Someone who forecasts failure and then fails is calibrated and unreliable. Their uncertainty is usable even though their work is not yet good, and for anyone deciding how much to check, that is worth a great deal.

The strong claim is that the four strokes are jointly necessary. Each "missing stroke" case can be made concrete.

Memorisation without prediction is intake with no spark. The learner stores what they are given and never finds out what they would have got wrong, so nothing marks which parts of their model need repair.

Practice without an outcome is a forecast that nothing meets. The theory predicts that repetition makes performance more fluent and confidence higher while accuracy stays where it was.

Feedback without a prior commitment is hindsight. The outcome arrives, but there is no recorded expectation for it to contradict, and the learner is free to feel that they knew it all along.

Fun and motivation without scoring supply energy and attention with no error signal. The learner enjoys the work, feels that they are learning, and cannot tell whether they are.

Scoring without consequence for the next attempt is audit. The record exists, and neither the learner's next forecast nor anyone's trust in them changes because of it.

Self-judged outcomes break the first rule. A learner who writes the result will, in time, learn to write the result.

The rest of this paper asks how much of this the evidence supports. We mark claims as established, contested, or our own conjecture.

## 2. What is established about the first three strokes

### Prediction error as the engine

The formal skeleton of the first two strokes is older than the theory and well developed. Rescorla and Wagner's (1972) model of conditioning made the change in an association proportional to the gap between what was expected and what occurred, so that a fully predicted event teaches nothing. Sutton (1988) generalised the idea in temporal-difference learning, in which credit is assigned by the difference between successive predictions and not only by the final result. Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997) then reported that midbrain dopamine neurons in monkeys fire in a pattern resembling the temporal-difference error: a burst for unpredicted reward, little response to predicted reward, and a dip when a predicted reward is withheld.

It is established that learning in many systems is driven by error and that dopamine activity often behaves like a reward prediction error. It is contested whether that description is complete. Jeong and colleagues (2022) reported nucleus accumbens dopamine measurements in mice that their retrospective causal-learning model fitted better than prediction-error models, and they presented this as a challenge to the dominant account. We found no settled resolution, and the conservative reading is that prediction error is a strong description of much of the data and not a closed question.

The wider programme known as predictive processing or the free-energy principle (Friston 2010; Clark 2013) proposes that the brain is at root a prediction machine and that perception, action and learning all minimise prediction error. We do not rest the four-stroke theory on it. Walsh and colleagues (2020), in a review that is broadly sympathetic, found the neurophysiological evidence suggestive and not conclusive, with many findings open to explanation by simpler mechanisms such as neural adaptation. Sun and Firestone (2020) pressed the "dark room" objection, that a system which only minimised surprise should seek out the most predictable place and stay there. Proponents have replied that exploration reduces error over the long run. The theory in this paper does not depend on that dispute. It needs only the more modest and better-supported claim that mismatch between expectation and result drives a large class of learning.

### Committing before the answer

The most direct support for the forecast stroke comes from experiments in which people are made to commit before they are told. Kornell, Hays and Bjork (2009) had participants try to answer questions built so that they would fail, and found that the failed attempt followed by the answer produced better later memory than studying the question and answer together for the same time. Richland, Kornell and Kao (2009) found the same pattern with a prose passage, and the benefit held when analysis was confined to items the participants had got wrong on the pretest. The outcome stroke matters here: Grimaldi and Karpicke (2012, as reported in the secondary literature we consulted) found the benefit disappeared when a short delay separated the failed attempt from the correct answer.

Butterfield and Metcalfe (2001) added confidence. People answered general-knowledge questions, rated their confidence, and received the correct answers. On a later test, errors that had been made with high confidence were more likely to have been corrected than errors made with low confidence. This "hypercorrection" effect has been replicated many times according to later reviews, and it fits the theory's central image closely. The larger the stated confidence, the larger the surprise when the outcome contradicts it, and the more is learned. Stahl and Feigenson (2015) found something similar in eleven-month-old infants, who learned more about objects that had violated their expectations, for instance by seeming to pass through a wall, and who chose to explore those objects in ways that tested the violated expectation. The generation effect, in which producing an item oneself yields better memory than reading it (Slamecka and Graf 1978), and the Bjorks' argument for desirable difficulties (Bjork and Bjork 2011) point the same way.

### Retrieval practice, and a finding that cuts against the theory

Roediger and Karpicke (2006) had students either restudy a prose passage or take a recall test on it. After five minutes the restudy group remembered more. After two days and after a week the tested group did. A meta-analysis by Rowland (2014) put the average advantage of testing over restudy at about half a standard deviation across 159 effect sizes, by the secondary summaries we were able to check.

This is usually cited in favour of theories like the present one, and in part it should be: reading again is the weakest of the options, which is the "memorisation is not enough" case. But the study contradicts a strict reading of the four-stroke claim. Roediger and Karpicke's participants received no feedback on their tests. Retention improved with no outcome stroke at all. The fair conclusion is that strengthening access to something one already knows is a different process from correcting a model of the world, and that only the second needs an outcome. We return to this in section 6.

### Feeling that one has learned

The "fun and motivation without scoring" case has a clean demonstration. Deslauriers and colleagues (2019) randomly assigned students in large introductory physics classes to active instruction or to polished lectures with identical content. Students in the active classes scored higher on a test of the material and rated their own learning lower. The authors attribute the gap partly to the effort that active learning demands, which students read as a sign that they are learning poorly. A felt sense of learning is therefore a poor proxy for learning, and can be negatively related to it.

This does not make motivation dispensable. Kang and colleagues (2009) found that curiosity about a trivia question tracked activity in reward-related brain regions and predicted better memory for the answer, especially when the participant's guess had been wrong. Theories of intrinsic motivation from robotics and machine learning go further and define the reward of curiosity as progress in prediction itself. Schmidhuber (2010) treats it as improvement in the learner's ability to compress or predict its experience, and Oudeyer, Kaplan and Hafner (2007) built a robot that sought situations where its prediction error was falling fastest and so moved by itself from easy activities to harder ones, avoiding both the trivially predictable and the hopelessly unpredictable. Gottlieb and colleagues (2013) review the neural and computational case that information seeking aims at reducing uncertainty. On these accounts well-founded fun is a by-product of the cycle running: it is what falling error feels like. That is an attractive unification and it remains a theoretical proposal. Špinka, Newberry and Bekoff (2001) proposed that animals at play deliberately put themselves in situations of lost control and so train for the unexpected, a hypothesis about function that later reviewers found incomplete.

### Hindsight

Fischhoff (1975) gave people accounts of unfamiliar historical events, told some of them how things had turned out, and asked all of them to judge how likely each possible outcome would have seemed beforehand. Those told the outcome rated it as having been more likely, and did not recognise that knowing it had changed their judgement. Roese and Vohs (2012) review several decades of work and distinguish three levels: misremembering one's earlier view, believing the event was inevitable, and believing one could have foreseen it. The replication record of the original experiment is mixed. A large replication by Chen and colleagues (2021) found the effect, and a replication database we consulted lists two later attempts as failures. The phenomenon across paradigms is nonetheless among the more robust in judgement research.

For the theory, hindsight bias is the reason a forecast must be recorded. Without a record, the outcome overwrites the expectation it should have been compared with, and the comparison that drives learning never occurs. The learner experiences feedback and feels informed while the surprise has been edited out.

### Scored forecasting

The scoring of probabilistic claims has a mature mathematics. Brier (1950) proposed scoring forecasts by the squared difference between the stated probability and what happened. Gneiting and Raftery (2007) set out the general theory of proper scoring rules, under which a forecaster does best in expectation by reporting what they actually believe. This matters to the theory in a specific way. A proper score makes honesty the best policy, so the confidence attached to a forecast becomes information and not posture.

Tetlock's long study of expert political forecasts (2005) is the standard evidence for what happens to judgement when it is never scored. By the accounts we could check, experts did poorly against simple baselines, differed by cognitive style, and were on average less accurate the more prominent they were. The forecasting tournaments that followed showed the other side. Mellers and colleagues (2014) reported that probability training, working in teams and grouping the best forecasters together each improved accuracy, and Mellers and colleagues (2015) reported that a small group of top performers kept their lead over two years and hundreds of questions when regression to the mean would have been expected. The first of these results is contested: Hauenstein and colleagues (2025) reanalysed the tournament data and found that controlling for extraneous variables reduced, removed or in some cases reversed the estimated effects of teaming and training. It still seems safe to say that people differ in forecasting skill, that the difference persists, and that it is visible only because the forecasts were scored.

### Practice and feedback

Kahneman and Klein (2009), who began from opposed positions on expert intuition, agreed on the conditions under which it can be trusted: an environment regular enough to be learned, and long practice with feedback. Where those are missing, confidence grows without skill.

Deliberate practice is the best-known claim in this area and has not held up at its original strength. Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993) reported that accumulated solitary practice distinguished violinists at three levels of accomplishment. Macnamara and Maitra (2019) repeated the study with a larger sample and tighter controls. Practice explained about a quarter of the variance in performance against roughly half in the original, and the best violinists had not practised more than the good ones. A meta-analysis by Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald (2014) found that deliberate practice explained about a quarter of performance variance in games, a fifth in music, and very little in education and the professions, figures that critics dispute on grounds of definition and measurement. Practice matters and is far from the whole story.

Feedback has its own surprise. Kluger and DeNisi (1996) pooled 607 effect sizes and found that feedback improved performance on average but made it worse in more than a third of cases. Their explanation was that feedback helps when it directs attention to the task and harms as it directs attention toward the self. This is close to independent support for the lesson stroke's rule that a lesson is about the task and not about the learner. It also shows that an outcome stroke, delivered badly, can do damage. The theory should claim that outcomes are necessary and should not claim that any outcome helps.

### Knowing what one knows

Fleming and Lau (2014) separate three things that a single confidence rating mixes together: bias, a general tendency to report high or low confidence; sensitivity, how well confidence distinguishes one's correct answers from one's errors; and efficiency, sensitivity relative to the underlying skill. Moore and Healy (2008) similarly split "overconfidence" into overestimating one's performance, overplacing oneself relative to others, and excessive certainty in one's beliefs, and found the third the most persistent.

These distinctions sharpen the theory. Correcting a bias is cheap, and a single remark that someone runs ten points high would do it. Sensitivity is the valuable part: knowing which of one's forecasts are the shaky ones. A general adjustment to the self cannot provide that, and a lesson about a kind of task can. The theory's insistence on task-level lessons is, on this reading, a claim about where improvement in metacognitive sensitivity comes from. That is our conjecture, and we know of no direct test of it.

## 3. The fourth stroke: trust, status and confidence

The first three strokes could be stated about a learner alone with a world. The fourth cannot. The claim under examination is that trust and status are how a group keeps score of its members' forecasts and commitments, that confidence is how a person keeps the same score from inside, and that developing trust and developing confidence are therefore one process seen from two sides. We take the pieces in turn.

### Trust as others' forecast about you

Mayer, Davis and Schoorman (1995), in the most cited model in organisational research, define trust as a willingness to be vulnerable to another party's actions, based on expectations about that party and without the ability to monitor or control them. They trace it to three perceived qualities: ability, benevolence and integrity. Two features of this definition match the theory. Trust is exposure accepted before the evidence is in, which is the theory's "what you are given ahead of the outcome". And trust rests on expectation, which makes it a forecast held by the truster about the trusted.

The three qualities also mark a limit. A record of scored forecasts speaks most directly to ability and, through promises kept, to integrity. It says less about benevolence, which is whether the other party means well toward you in particular. A theory of trust built only on calibration would miss the case of the competent, accurate adversary. Any honest statement of the fourth stroke has to say that track record is one input to trust and not the whole of it.

### Children keep score

Developmental work shows the score being kept early. Koenig, Clément and Harris (2004) showed three- and four-year-olds two informants, one who named familiar objects correctly and one who named them wrongly. Children who could say which informant had been wrong then preferred the accurate informant's names for unfamiliar objects. Harris and colleagues (2018) review a large body of later work showing that children track informants' past accuracy and that older children also weigh what an informant was in a position to know, along with group membership and consensus, and sometimes defer for social reasons where the evidence would not justify it.

Sperber and colleagues (2010) argue that this is a human universal: because communication exposes us to misinformation, we have mechanisms of "epistemic vigilance" that assess both the content of a message and the competence and goodwill of its source. Mercier (2017) reviewed evidence from persuasion, propaganda and advertising and concluded that people are a good deal less gullible than is commonly supposed, with mass persuasion mostly succeeding where the message fits what the audience already believes. This remains an argued position and not a consensus.

### Confidence as a stake

The proposal that expressed confidence is a publicly staked forecast has direct experimental support. Tenney, Spellman and MacCoun (2008) found that observers judging witnesses took confidence as a sign of accuracy when they had nothing else to go on, and that once they could see how well a witness's confidence tracked their accuracy, credibility followed calibration, with neither confidence nor accuracy alone accounting for it. A witness who is confident and wrong loses more than one who is hesitant and wrong. Vullioud and colleagues (2017) stated the mechanism in evolutionary terms: expressing confidence commits the speaker. Confident messages are more readily accepted, and confident speakers pay a higher reputational price when the message proves false. The asymmetry is what keeps the signal honest enough to be worth attending to. The argument belongs with costly-signalling theory (Zahavi 1975), whose generality remains debated in biology, and with work on reputation as a basis for cooperation among strangers (Nowak and Sigmund 2005).

On this evidence the first half of the conjecture stands. A statement of confidence functions socially as a wager, and listeners settle it.

### Status as a running score, and where that fails

Henrich and Gil-White (2001) distinguished two routes to high rank. Dominance is deference taken by force or threat. Prestige is deference freely given to people whose skill or knowledge others want access to, and they argued that it evolved because learners benefit from attending to the most successful models. Cheng and colleagues (2013) found in small task groups that both routes independently predicted who ended up with influence. Anderson, Hildreth and Howland (2015) reviewed the literature and concluded that the desire for status, defined as respect and voluntary deference, is a fundamental motive and not a by-product of the need to belong.

Prestige is the part of status that could be a running score of whose commitments have paid off. Three bodies of evidence say the score is kept loosely.

First, dominance works as well. Cheng and colleagues' result is that intimidation is a viable route to influence beside competence. A good part of human rank has nothing to do with being right.

Second, prestige follows cues and not records. Jiménez and Mesoudi (2019), reviewing the evidence on prestige-biased learning, note open questions about how well prestige tracks competence. A later online experiment (Brand and colleagues 2020) found that people used prestige cues when direct information about success was unavailable. That is sensible and it is also the gap in the ledger: where the record cannot be seen, the group scores the proxy.

Third, and most damaging, overconfidence pays. Anderson and colleagues (2012) found across six studies that people who overrated their own ability attained higher status in their groups, apparently because they came across as competent, and that wanting status made people more overconfident. Kennedy, Anderson and Moore (2013) then tested the obvious corrective, revealing to the group that a person's confidence had been unjustified, and reported that the status benefits outweighed any costs. Tenney and colleagues (2019) refined the picture: overconfident people were penalised after exposure when their confidence had been expressed in explicit, checkable words, and escaped when it had been expressed through manner, posture and tone, which leave deniability. Von Hippel and Trivers (2011) offer a theory of why the strategy might be built in, proposing that we deceive ourselves so as to deceive others more convincingly. That proposal drew wide disagreement in the commentary published with it.

So the conjecture that status is the group's score of forecasts is true only of explicit, falsifiable commitments, and human groups do much of their ranking on signals that were never forecasts in that sense. What the theory can still say is narrower and, we think, more useful: status tracks calibration to the degree that commitments are made in checkable words and the outcomes are visible. Where they are not, the fourth stroke runs on display. This is a prediction and it could be tested by varying the visibility of records across otherwise similar groups.

### The inner gauge

Leary and colleagues (1995) proposed that self-esteem is a sociometer, an internal gauge of how far one is included or excluded by others, and reported five studies in support. If so, a person's feeling about themselves is already a reading of their social ledger. Mahadevan and colleagues (2016) proposed a rival, the hierometer, on which self-regard tracks status and not inclusion, and reported two large cross-sectional studies that consistently supported the status account and gave mixed support to the inclusion account. Both theories agree that self-regard is an internal estimate of external standing, and they disagree about which standing. Neither shows that the gauge is calibrated. Both lines of evidence are largely correlational.

The theory's claim is stronger than either. It says that confidence ought to be earned by the same record that earns others' trust, and that learning is what brings it into line. The evidence supports a weaker statement: people carry an internal estimate of how others regard them, it moves with social outcomes, and it is frequently miscalibrated in the upward direction (Moore and Healy 2008). "Confidence is trust turned inward" describes what the gauge appears to be for. It does not yet describe how the gauge behaves.

### What makes a forecast affordable

A forecast exposes the forecaster, and several literatures bear on what makes that exposure tolerable.

Attachment theory holds that a dependable caregiver serves as a secure base from which a child explores (Bowlby 1988). In adults, Elliot and Reis (2003) found across four studies that secure attachment was associated with stronger need for achievement and less fear of failure, and Feeney and Thrush (2010) observed married couples and found that a partner's availability, encouragement and non-interference predicted the other's exploration. These are associations and not experiments. The reading we propose is that security lowers the cost of being wrong, and being wrong is the cost that every forecast risks. That reading is conjecture.

Learned helplessness is the classic account of what happens when outcomes stop depending on what one does, and its authors have revised it. Maier and Seligman (2016) concluded from fifty years of work, much of it on the neural circuitry in rats, that the original theory "got it backwards". Passivity in the face of prolonged, uncontrollable adversity is the default, produced by a brainstem serotonergic system. What is learned is control: when the prefrontal cortex detects that action changes outcomes, it inhibits that default and can establish an expectation of control that protects against later adversity. Some commentators dispute whether learning has been ruled out of the passive response, and the extension to humans is inferential. If the revision holds, it restates the theory's fourth stroke in biological terms. Agency is built by experiencing that one's acts move outcomes, and is not a resting state that failure wears away. A learner whose forecasts never meet outcomes that depend on them stays at the default instead of losing a confidence they once had.

At the level of the group, Edmondson (1999) studied 51 work teams and found that psychological safety, a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk, was associated with learning behaviour such as asking for help and admitting error, which in turn was associated with performance. A meta-analysis of 136 samples (Frazier and colleagues 2017) confirmed associations with learning and performance outcomes. The original study is cross-sectional and from one company. The condition it describes is the one under which members can afford the first stroke in public.

### Why people will not commit

If forecasts are so useful, their rarity needs explaining. Our account is conjecture assembled from findings that were not designed to test it. A recorded forecast makes a possible failure attributable. Berglas and Jones (1978) showed that people who had succeeded without knowing why chose, before a retest, a drug they believed would impair them. The result held for men and not for women in their studies, and later replications were mixed. The proposed motive is to arrange in advance that failure will say nothing about ability. Declining to forecast is the cheapest handicap of all: with no stated expectation there is nothing to be wrong about. Kluger and DeNisi's finding that feedback harms when it turns attention to the self suggests what is being avoided. Tenney and colleagues' finding that checkable verbal confidence is punished while vague confident manner is not shows that the avoidance is, in ordinary social life, a rational response to incentives.

The avoidance of the forecast stroke is therefore not well described as a personal failing, and a setting that wants forecasts has to change the payoff.

### One process seen from two sides?

The evidence supports the following. Listeners treat confidence as a commitment and settle it when they can. Children keep accuracy records on informants. Trust is by definition exposure accepted on the strength of an expectation. Self-regard moves with social standing. The capacity to act with an expectation of control is acquired through controllable outcomes. In each case an inner quantity and an outer one are estimates of the same thing, namely how a person's commitments tend to turn out.

The evidence does not support the claim that the two estimates agree, or that social life generally pushes them to. Groups reward confidence displays that no record backs. Self-assessments are inflated. Trust depends on perceived goodwill as well as on record. Dominance buys rank without accuracy.

We conclude that trust and confidence are the same process seen from outside and from inside only where the four strokes are in fact running: where commitments are explicit, outcomes are independent and visible, and the record is readable by the person it concerns. Under those conditions both are driven by one ledger and should converge. Elsewhere they drift apart, and the usual direction of drift favours display. This is the paper's main conjecture, and it is a claim about what the cycle does when present and not about what people ordinarily do.

## 4. Artificial learners

The first three strokes each have a clear counterpart in machine learning. Much of modern training consists of having a system predict something withheld from it and adjusting it by the error, and reinforcement learning is built on the temporal-difference error described above (Sutton 1988). The standard training loss for classifiers and language models is the logarithmic score, one of the proper scoring rules analysed by Gneiting and Raftery (2007), so such systems are trained under a rule that rewards honest probabilities.

Whether the resulting confidence is honest is an empirical matter. Guo and colleagues (2017) found that the deep networks of that period were poorly calibrated, typically overconfident, in a way earlier and smaller networks had not been, and that a simple correction after training repaired much of the problem. For language models, Kadavath and colleagues (2022) reported that large models were well calibrated on multiple-choice and true-or-false questions in suitable formats and could partly predict whether they knew an answer, with weaker calibration on unfamiliar tasks. Xiong and colleagues (2024) found that when models were asked to state their confidence in words, they were strongly overconfident, with stated values clustering between 80 and 100 per cent.

The rule against marking one's own work has direct analogues. Amodei and colleagues (2016) describe reward hacking, in which a system finds a way to raise its measured score that the designer did not intend, as a core safety problem. This is the engineering form of the self-authored outcome. Panickssery, Bowman and Feng (2024) found that language models used as evaluators could recognise their own outputs at better than chance and rated them more favourably than human judges thought warranted, with the degree of self-recognition related to the degree of self-preference. A system grading its own work, or grading the work of a near copy of itself, is a weaker teacher than it appears.

What follows is speculation and we label it so. A system that learns by changing what is in front of it at the time of acting, and not by changing its parameters, changes its behaviour only when that context changes. For such a system the lesson stroke would be something written into future context, and the theory would predict that a short record of task-level lessons, each tied to the forecasts that justified it, does more than a number describing the system's general overconfidence. It would also predict that a lesson worth keeping survives replacement of the underlying model, since a lesson that stops holding after a replacement was a fact about the occupant and not about the work. We know of no published test of either prediction.

Whether an artificial system could develop something that deserves to be called confidence or trust is unclear, and the evidence here does not settle it. The functional question is tractable: whether a system's stated uncertainty is usable by those who depend on it, and whether the latitude it is given follows its record. On that reading, an artificial system that can be trusted is one whose forecasts are scored by something it cannot influence, whose record it can read, and whose scope widens or narrows with that record and with the stakes. Whether anything is felt by such a system as its record improves is a separate question that this paper cannot address.

## 5. Objections and limits

The strong claim was that all learning requires all four strokes. Several well-established kinds of learning do not fit.

Statistical learning proceeds without any stated forecast or external marker. Saffran, Aslin and Newport (1996) showed that eight-month-old infants, after two minutes of exposure to a continuous stream of syllables, distinguished sequences that had occurred together reliably from those that had not. Reber (1967) showed that adults who memorised letter strings generated by hidden rules could later classify new strings at better than chance while unable to state the rules. One can reply that such learners are forecasting implicitly and that the next syllable is the outcome. The reply saves the theory at the cost of its content. If any internal expectation counts as a forecast and any subsequent input as an outcome, then error-driven learning has the first two strokes by definition and the claim that they are necessary is no longer a discovery. Much unsupervised learning in machines fits this description: the forecast and the outcome are both present and neither is public.

Retrieval practice without feedback, already discussed, strengthens memory with no outcome. Sleep consolidates memory with no new experience at all (Diekelmann and Born 2010), and Wagner and colleagues (2004) found that sleep roughly doubled the proportion of people who discovered a hidden shortcut in a task they had practised. Insight of this kind is a restructuring that happens offline. It resembles the lesson stroke more than the others, in that a generalisation appears that lets particulars be discarded, but it arrives without a fresh forecast or outcome.

Observational learning lets a learner profit from forecasts they did not make. Burke and colleagues (2010) found, in a brain-imaging study of people learning by watching another player, activity corresponding to two distinct errors, one for the other person's action and one for the other person's outcome. This suggests that the observer runs a version of the cycle on another's behalf, and we cannot rule out that this is redescription. Bandura's (1965) study of children imitating a filmed adult is generally reported as showing that the behaviour was acquired whether or not the model was seen to be rewarded, with reward affecting only whether it was performed. We verified the citation and relied on secondary accounts for the finding.

Some learning is prepared in advance or occurs on very little evidence. Garcia and Koelling (1966) showed that rats readily associated taste with later illness and sound and light with shock, and not the reverse, which means that what can be learned from an outcome depends on evolved structure the learner brings to it. People can learn a new visual concept from one example, and Lake, Salakhutdinov and Tenenbaum (2015) matched that with a model that builds concepts as small generative programs from rich prior structure. Neither result removes prediction and outcome from learning, but both show how much a prior can do before the cycle starts.

"Universal" does not survive in its strong form. What survives is narrower and still a real claim. We suggest that the four strokes are jointly necessary for one kind of learning in particular: the acquisition of calibrated judgement that others can rely upon. Memory, perceptual tuning and skill fluency can each improve with fewer strokes. A person's knowledge of how far to trust their own judgement, and other people's knowledge of how far to trust that person, cannot. That kind of learning concerns the relation between confidence and results, which is undefined without a prior confidence, an independent result, a record that connects them, and a consequence that depends on the record.

Narrowed this way, the theory is falsifiable. It would be wrong if people or machines reliably improved the discrimination of their confidence in a domain, without transfer from a scored one, when they received no independent outcomes. It would be wrong if recording forecasts before feedback added nothing to feedback alone with time and exposure held equal. The pretesting and hypercorrection studies suggest it does add something, for memory at least, and the test for judgement is less complete. It would be wrong if self-scored practice produced the same gains as independently scored practice over a long run, or if groups with full and visible records of explicit commitments went on awarding status to displayed confidence at the rate found by Kennedy and colleagues. And the lesson stroke would be idle if task-level written lessons added nothing to raw scored feedback. That last test has not, to our knowledge, been run, and it is the weakest joint in the theory.

The theory also makes predictions that neighbouring accounts do not. Pure practice and pure feedback accounts are indifferent to whether the learner committed first. A motivation account predicts that enjoyment and learning rise together, where this theory predicts that they separate whenever scoring is absent, as Deslauriers and colleagues found. A pure prediction-error account covers the first two strokes and is silent on why a lesson should concern the task and not the self, and on why the social allocation of latitude belongs to learning at all. The four-stroke theory predicts specifically that the convergence of self-confidence and others' trust depends on the visibility of records, and that calibration separates from reliability in ways that matter to anyone who delegates.

## 6. Implications

For teaching, the first implication is to ask for the forecast before giving the answer. A question attempted before instruction, a prediction written down before a demonstration, or a confidence mark beside each answer costs little and has experimental support. The answer should follow promptly. The second is to keep the learner from marking their own work while making sure they get the marked paper back, which is ordinary good practice restated. The third is to direct feedback to the task: which kinds of problem went wrong and under what conditions, and not what kind of student this is. The fourth concerns what students are told about themselves. A feeling of fluency does not show that learning has occurred, effortful methods feel worse and work better, and students who know this in advance may resist those methods less. A fifth, more tentative, is to treat a calibrated "I do not know this yet" as an achievement in its own right. The retrieval-practice findings add a caution. Not everything needs the full cycle, and for consolidating what is already known, plain self-testing works.

For institutions, the theory explains a familiar pathology. Organisations are rich in commitments that nobody scores, such as plans, estimates and strategies, and in scores that change nothing, such as audits and reviews. The remedies are structural: record commitments in checkable words with a date, have someone other than the author settle them, keep the record open to the person it concerns, and let latitude follow the record in proportion to the stakes. A good record should mean being checked after the act instead of before it. It should not mean going unchecked, since a second perspective finds what the first cannot, whoever the first is. Institutions should also expect resistance: where a clear advance commitment is the only form of confidence that can be punished, people will sensibly avoid making one, and psychological safety in Edmondson's sense is the precondition for the first stroke being offered at all. And if status in a group follows confident manner more than checkable record, as the evidence suggests it often does, then making records visible is the lever most likely to bring the two back together.

For tools, the same four requirements apply. A tool that supports learning asks for the expectation first, with a rough confidence. It closes the loop by returning to the user what happened against what they said, promptly and specifically. It keeps a short notebook of lessons in the user's own words, each linked to the cases behind it, and shows it at the point of the next decision and not in a report nobody opens. And it holds nothing about the user that the user cannot read. The same design applies where the learner is an automated system: forecasts with stated probabilities before action, outcomes settled by something the system cannot influence and does not share blind spots with, lessons written about the task and carried forward, and scope that follows the record. The hardest case in both settings is the judge that can be read and written toward. A scoring rubric that the learner can see and optimise against is a teacher that can be flattered, and the less an outcome resembles a plain fact about the world, the more care it needs.

## 7. Conclusion

The four-stroke theory is at its strongest as a theory of earned judgement. Its first two strokes rest on some of the most secure findings in the study of learning, that surprise teaches and that an expectation recorded in advance is what makes surprise possible. Its third stroke is plausible, has indirect support in the feedback and metacognition literatures, and has not been tested directly. Its fourth stroke is its distinctive contribution and its most exposed. Trust does behave like a forecast others hold about a person, confidence does behave like a commitment others will settle, and agency does seem to be built from outcomes that answer to one's acts. But human groups keep the ledger carelessly and pay for display. The claim worth keeping is conditional: where forecasts are explicit, outcomes independent, and records readable, confidence and trust are one quantity and learning is what moves it. Building such conditions is the practical programme the theory recommends. Whether they are sufficient is an open empirical question.
