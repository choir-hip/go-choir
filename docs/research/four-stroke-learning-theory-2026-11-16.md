# Call it, check it, learn it, earn it: four strokes always running wherever learning works, with the outcome first among them

*Draft prepared with an AI research assistant*

## Abstract

This paper states a diagnostic theory of learning and tests it against the published record. Wherever learning is working, four strokes are being performed in some form: a forecast, an outcome the learner did not author, a lesson, and a change in trust. They are not steps taken in turn. They run concurrently, at several timescales, and each may be explicit, implicit, partial or done by proxy. The weakest bounds the rate of learning, and the outcome is claimed to matter most, because contact with something the learner does not author is the one input the others cannot manufacture. Where learning stalls, the theory says to look for a stroke that is absent, degraded, denied or faked. The evidence supports much of this: error-driven learning, the benefit of committing first, the weak accuracy of self-assessment, the dependence of expertise on valid feedback. It strains elsewhere. Retrieval practice works without external feedback, self-belief follows performance more than it drives it, and groups reward displayed confidence. No study ranks the strokes directly. We separate implicit strokes that have independent evidence from those merely posited, state predictions that could fail, and end with a four-question diagnostic.

## 1. The theory

Most accounts of learning name one ingredient, such as practice, feedback, motivation or memory, and treat it as the whole. The theory examined here says that learning has four components, that all four are in effect wherever learning is working, and that when one of them is not occurring, learning stalls. Following the engineering image in which the theory was first put, we call them strokes. The short form is: call it, check it, learn it, earn it.

The first stroke is the forecast. Before acting, the learner expects something. In the fullest form they say what they expect, how sure they are, and what observation would settle it. A forecast is a claim that can turn out wrong, held at a time when the learner does not yet know whether it will.

The second stroke is the outcome. Something says what happened. In the fullest form that something is not the learner, and the theory attaches a rule: you do not mark your own work. The one who made the forecast does not settle it, and the one whose act is being judged cannot write the result. Outcomes differ in how well they teach. A good one is prompt, specific and cannot be flattered.

The third stroke is the lesson. When the outcome meets the forecast, the learner takes away something general that changes the next forecast. In the fullest form they write it down, in their own words, about the task and not about themselves, with a pointer to the record that justified it. Most outcomes confirm an existing lesson, and new lessons come from surprises.

The fourth stroke is trust. Trust is what the learner is given ahead of the next outcome: more room, larger stakes, less checking before the act, or the reverse. It is given by others and it is also turned inward, as the learner's confidence. In the fullest form it follows the record, growing when forecasts were called and met and shrinking when they were not. Trust carries what was earned into the next cycle, and because a lesson is weighed according to its source, it joins the fourth stroke back to the first.

A second rule governs the whole cycle: nothing is held about you that you cannot read. The learner gets the marked paper back. Readable does not mean writable. Independence, and not secrecy, is the defence against gaming.

A scored forecast yields two quantities with two different readers. Calibration asks whether the stated confidence was honest, and its reader is the learner. Reliability asks whether the result met the standard the other party set, and its reader is whoever depends on the learner. Someone who forecasts failure and then fails is calibrated and unreliable. Their uncertainty is usable even though their work is not yet good.

### Concurrent, not sequential

The strokes are listed in an order and are not performed in one. A working learner is forecasting, meeting outcomes, revising lessons and gaining or losing trust all the time and at several timescales at once: within a single attempt, across a week of attempts, across a career. A lesson drawn from last month's outcomes shapes this minute's forecast while this minute's outcome is already arriving. Here the engine image stops being useful. A real four-stroke engine is strictly ordered, each stroke beginning where the last ended, and learning is not like that. What the image keeps is that each stroke does work the others cannot and that the whole produces motion and not mere repetition.

A closer model is the decision loop of the military strategist John Boyd, usually given as observe, orient, decide, act. Boyd left briefings and no paper. His only diagram of the loop, in a short briefing dated 1995 and revised in 1996, is not the circle of four steps that popular accounts draw. By the descriptions we consulted, orientation sits at the centre and shapes observation, decision and action together, with feedback paths throughout and "implicit guidance and control" paths by which orientation drives action without passing through an explicit decision. Osinga's (2007) study of Boyd argues that the common reading, a cycle to be completed faster than an opponent's, is an incomplete account of his thought. We checked the archive record of the briefing and secondary descriptions of the diagram, which differ in detail, and did not study the slides themselves. The parallel to the present theory is close: components that overlap and feed each other continuously, and paths that work without an explicit step.

One ordering constraint remains, and it is logical and not temporal. For any given claim, the forecast must be fixed before its outcome is known. An expectation formed or altered after the result cannot be scored by that result. Everything else may overlap.

### The outcome comes first in importance

The theory holds that the strokes are not equal and that the outcome matters most. The argument is that the outcome is the only stroke that brings in something from outside the learner. A forecast, a lesson and a level of confidence can each be produced by the learner alone, well or badly. Contact with a result the learner did not author cannot be. It is the scarce input, and the other three are operations on it. It follows that the others can be implicit, partial or by proxy at less cost than the outcome can. Section 3 asks how far the evidence supports this ranking.

### What the theory claims and what it does not

The theory does not say that learning requires four explicit, recorded steps. Most learning plainly has no such thing. It says that each stroke is always being realised in one of four ways. It may be explicit, as when a forecaster writes down a probability and an examiner marks the result. It may be implicit, as when a nervous system generates an expectation that nobody states. It may be partial, as when feedback arrives late or covers only some of what was attempted. Or it may be done by proxy, as when a learner with no outside verifier scores their own work, or a group with no record of someone's forecasts judges them by their manner.

The claim has a bottleneck structure. The rate of learning is bounded by the weakest stroke, in the way that the narrowest section bounds the flow through a pipe. A strong outcome signal is wasted on a learner with no recoverable forecast to compare it with. A sharp forecast is wasted where nothing settles it. Both are wasted if nothing general is taken away, or if what is carried forward bears no relation to what happened.

It follows that the theory is a diagnostic and not a checklist. Where learning has stalled, it says to look for the stroke that is absent, degraded, denied or faked. A stroke is absent when nothing performs it. It is degraded when its proxy is poor: self-scoring by a learner who cannot judge the work, feedback too delayed or noisy to use. It is denied when the learner has access to it and refuses it: the forecast revised after the fact, the miss not admitted. It is faked when a substitute is produced that looks like the stroke and carries none of its information: the outcome written by the actor, the credit taken despite the miss.

Sections 2 to 5 take the strokes in turn and ask, of each, how it is realised, how it fails, and what the evidence says. Section 6 considers artificial learners. Section 7 faces the objection that a theory allowing implicit strokes cannot be wrong. We mark claims throughout as established, contested, or our own conjecture.

## 2. The forecast stroke

### Implicit forecasts for which there is independent evidence

The formal skeleton of the first two strokes is older than this theory. Rescorla and Wagner's (1972) model of conditioning made the change in an association proportional to the gap between what was expected and what occurred, so that a fully predicted event teaches nothing. Sutton (1988) generalised the idea in temporal-difference learning, in which credit is assigned by the difference between successive predictions. Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997) reported that midbrain dopamine neurons in monkeys fire in a pattern resembling that error: a burst for unpredicted reward, little response to predicted reward, and a dip when a predicted reward is withheld. Here an unstated forecast was measured directly, in the dip that occurs when nothing happens at the moment something was expected.

It is established that much learning is driven by error and that dopamine activity often behaves like a reward prediction error. Whether that description is complete is contested. Jeong and colleagues (2022) reported dopamine measurements in mice that a retrospective causal-learning model fitted better than prediction-error models. We found no settled resolution.

Implicit forecasts can be measured in infants too. Emberson, Richards and Aslin (2015) trained six-month-olds to expect a picture after a sound and then omitted the picture. Visual cortex responded on the omission trials, which the authors read as expectation-based feedback already present at that age. Stahl and Feigenson (2015) found that eleven-month-olds learned more about objects that had violated their expectations, for instance by seeming to pass through a wall, and chose to explore those objects in ways that tested the violated expectation.

The wider programme known as predictive processing or the free-energy principle (Friston 2010; Clark 2013) proposes that the brain is at root a prediction machine. We do not rest the theory on it. Walsh and colleagues (2020), in a broadly sympathetic review, found the neurophysiological evidence suggestive and not conclusive, with many findings open to simpler explanations such as neural adaptation. Sun and Firestone (2020) pressed the "dark room" objection, that a system minimising surprise should stay in the most predictable place. The present theory needs only the narrower claim that mismatch between expectation and result drives a large class of learning.

### What making the forecast explicit adds

If implicit forecasts are everywhere, the question is what explicit ones add. The experimental answer is that they add a good deal. Kornell, Hays and Bjork (2009) had participants try to answer questions built so that they would fail, and found that a failed attempt followed by the answer produced better later memory than studying question and answer together for the same time. Richland, Kornell and Kao (2009) found the same with a prose passage, and the benefit held for items the participants had got wrong on the pretest. Butterfield and Metcalfe (2001) added confidence. People answered general-knowledge questions, rated their confidence and were shown the correct answers. Errors made with high confidence were more likely to be corrected on a later test than errors made with low confidence. The larger the stated confidence, the larger the surprise, and the more was learned.

Curiosity research connects the forecast to motivation. Kang and colleagues (2009) found that curiosity about a trivia question tracked activity in reward-related brain regions and predicted better memory for the answer, especially when the participant's guess had been wrong. Theories of intrinsic motivation define the reward of curiosity as progress in prediction (Schmidhuber 2010; Oudeyer, Kaplan and Hafner 2007; reviewed by Gottlieb and colleagues 2013), so that well-founded enjoyment is what falling error feels like. That remains a theoretical proposal, as does the idea that animal play is training for the unexpected (Špinka, Newberry and Bekoff 2001).

### The forecast denied: hindsight

The first way the stroke fails is that the learner has a forecast and afterwards misstates it. Fischhoff (1975) gave people accounts of unfamiliar historical events, told some of them how things had turned out, and asked all of them how likely each outcome would have seemed beforehand. Those told the outcome rated it as more likely and did not recognise that knowing it had changed their judgement. Roese and Vohs (2012) distinguish three levels: misremembering one's earlier view, believing the event was inevitable, and believing one could have foreseen it. The replication record of the original experiment is mixed. A large replication by Chen and colleagues (2021) found the effect, and a replication database we consulted lists two later failures. Across paradigms the phenomenon is among the more robust in judgement research.

For the theory this is the central case of a stroke present and then denied. The forecast existed. The outcome overwrote it, the comparison never occurred, and the learner experienced feedback while the surprise was edited out. Roese and Vohs list motivational as well as cognitive sources, so some of this is honest memory failure and some is unwillingness to have been wrong. A recorded forecast repairs both because it replaces a poor proxy, the remembered expectation, with a better one.

### The forecast withheld

The second failure is that the learner declines to commit. A recorded forecast makes a possible failure attributable. Berglas and Jones (1978) showed that people who had succeeded without knowing why chose, before a retest, a drug they believed would impair them. The result held for men and not for women in their studies, and later replications were mixed. The proposed motive is to arrange in advance that failure will say nothing about ability. Declining to forecast is the cheapest handicap of all, since with no stated expectation there is nothing to be wrong about.

The social incentives point the same way. Tenney and colleagues (2019) found that confidence expressed in explicit, checkable words was punished when it proved wrong, while confidence expressed through manner and tone was not. In ordinary life a clear advance commitment is the form of confidence most easily held against a person. Edmondson (1999) studied 51 work teams and found that psychological safety, a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk, was associated with learning behaviour such as admitting error, and through it with performance. A meta-analysis of 136 samples (Frazier and colleagues 2017) confirmed the associations. Attachment research suggests a similar condition for individuals: a dependable caregiver serves as a secure base for exploration (Bowlby 1988), secure adults report less fear of failure (Elliot and Reis 2003), and a partner's encouragement predicted exploration in observed couples (Feeney and Thrush 2010). These studies are correlational. Our reading, that safety and security lower the cost of being wrong and so make the forecast affordable, is conjecture, as is the role of shame, on which we cite no direct evidence.

## 3. The outcome stroke

### External outcomes and their quality

The explicit form of the stroke has a mature mathematics. Brier (1950) proposed scoring forecasts by the squared difference between the stated probability and what happened. Gneiting and Raftery (2007) set out the theory of proper scoring rules, under which a forecaster does best by reporting what they actually believe. A proper score makes honesty the best policy, so stated confidence becomes information and not posture.

Tetlock's long study of expert political forecasts (2005) is the standard evidence for what happens when judgement is never scored. By the accounts we could check, experts did poorly against simple baselines and were on average less accurate the more prominent they were. In the forecasting tournaments that followed, Mellers and colleagues (2015) reported that a small group of top performers kept their lead over two years and hundreds of questions. Mellers and colleagues (2014) had reported that training and teaming improved accuracy. That result is contested: Hauenstein and colleagues (2025) reanalysed the data and found that controlling for extraneous variables reduced, removed or sometimes reversed those effects.

Outcomes can be present and still teach badly. Kahneman and Klein (2009), who began from opposed positions on expert intuition, agreed that it can be trusted only where the environment is regular enough to be learned and the practitioner has had long practice with feedback. Hogarth's distinction between kind and wicked learning environments makes the same point structurally. In the 2015 statement (Hogarth, Lejarraga and Soyer), an environment is kind when the information available where one learns matches the information that matters where one must act, and wicked when they diverge. Secondary accounts of Hogarth's earlier work describe wicked settings as those where feedback is absent, delayed or inaccurate. We did not verify that wording in a primary source. In a wicked environment the outcome stroke is partial or misleading, and experience then produces confidence without skill.

An outcome can also do harm. Kluger and DeNisi (1996) pooled 607 effect sizes and found that feedback improved performance on average and made it worse in more than a third of cases. We return to their explanation under the lesson stroke.

### Self-scoring as a proxy, and why it is a bottleneck

Often there is no external verifier and the learner must score their own work. The theory treats this as a proxy for the outcome stroke, and usually a poor one. The evidence on self-assessment bears this out. Zell and Krizan (2014) combined 22 meta-analyses comparing people's evaluations of their own ability with objective measures of performance. The mean correlation was .29, with a range from .09 to .63. Correspondence was better when the self-evaluation was specific and the task was objective, familiar or simple. Dunning, Heath and Suls (2004) reviewed the same problem in health, education and work, and noted that other people's predictions of a person's outcomes were sometimes more accurate than the person's own, and that people tend to be overconfident in newly learned skills.

Three mechanisms explain why the proxy is weak. The first is that the learner often grades with the answer in view. Koriat and Bjork (2005) showed that when people judge how well they have learned a pair of words while both are in front of them, their judgement is driven by how related the words look together and not by how likely the first is to call up the second alone. They called this a foresight bias. The second is that fluency passes for mastery. Deslauriers and colleagues (2019) randomly assigned physics students to active instruction or to polished lectures with identical content. The active classes scored higher on a test and rated their own learning lower. The third, which is contested, is that the skill needed to do a task is also the skill needed to judge it, so that a learner who cannot yet do the work is writing their own answer key without the knowledge to write it. Kruger and Dunning (1999) drew this conclusion from the finding that the poorest performers overestimated themselves most. Gignac and Zajenkowski (2020) argued that the pattern is mostly a statistical artefact of regression to the mean and the general tendency to rate oneself above average, and their analysis has been disputed in turn. We rest nothing on it. The Zell and Krizan figure does not depend on it.

Self-scoring is therefore a constraint on the rate of learning and not a disqualification. A correlation of .29 carries some information, and of .63 a good deal. The theory predicts that a self-scoring learner learns, slowly, and fastest where the task is concrete enough for self-assessment to be accurate.

### The outcome faked

A learner who can write the result may, over time, learn to write the result. The human evidence for this is the self-enhancement literature discussed under the fourth stroke. The clearest demonstrations are in machines, where the phenomenon has a name (section 6).

### Is the outcome really the most important stroke?

The theory says so, and the evidence bears on the claim without settling it.

Three lines of evidence favour it. The first is that the conditions under which experience produces skill, as far as they have been identified, are conditions on the outcome. Kahneman and Klein's agreed list is a learnable environment and feedback. Hogarth's kind and wicked environments are defined by the information the world returns. Neither names the learner's forecasting habits, note-taking or confidence. The second is the self-assessment evidence. When the outcome is supplied by the learner, it agrees only weakly with an independent measure, and nothing the learner does with a forecast or a lesson can restore information the outcome never contained. The third comes from machine learning, discussed in section 6, where the central discipline is evaluation on data the system has not seen and cannot influence, and where the characteristic failures are of a system finding a way to author its own score.

There is also an asymmetry in how well the strokes survive being implicit. The forecast works implicitly across the animal kingdom, on the evidence in section 2, and making it explicit adds a benefit to learning that was already occurring. We know of no comparable evidence that an implicit or self-supplied outcome serves nearly as well as an external one.

Against this, two findings resist. Retrieval practice improves retention with no external outcome at all, which shows that for some kinds of learning the outcome can be internal. And in the pretesting studies, a forecast added to an unchanged outcome produced a clear gain, which shows that the outcome is not sufficient and that a missing forecast is also costly.

Most importantly, we found no study that degrades each stroke in turn, in the same task and on comparable learners, and compares the damage. Without one, the ranking is the theory's claim and not a finding. The test is straightforward to describe. Take a judgement task with a real criterion. Cross four manipulations: forecast recorded or not, outcome external or self-scored, lesson prompted at task level or not, and latitude tied to record or fixed. The primacy claim predicts that the outcome manipulation has the largest effect on the learning rate, and that the other three matter much more when the outcome is external than when it is self-scored.

### An outcome that seems to be absent

Roediger and Karpicke (2006) had students either restudy a prose passage or take a recall test on it. After five minutes the restudy group remembered more. After two days and after a week the tested group did. The tested students received no feedback. A meta-analysis by Rowland (2014) put the average advantage of testing over restudy at about half a standard deviation, by the secondary summaries we could check. On its face this is learning with no outcome stroke. Section 7 takes up whether an implicit outcome can fairly be claimed.

## 4. The lesson stroke

The lesson is the stroke with the least direct evidence, and the theory's claims about it are the most specific.

### Task-level and self-level lessons

Kluger and DeNisi (1996) explained their finding by where feedback directs attention. Feedback helped when it kept attention on the task and its details. It lost effect, and could harm, as it moved attention toward the self. This is close to independent support for the theory's rule. "Estimates ran short when the job had hidden dependencies" is a task-level lesson. "You are overconfident" is a self-level one.

The metacognition literature says why the difference should matter. Fleming and Lau (2014) separate three things that a confidence rating mixes together: bias, a general tendency to report high or low confidence; sensitivity, how well confidence distinguishes one's correct answers from one's errors; and efficiency, sensitivity relative to skill. A self-level lesson can correct bias and nothing else. It shifts every future forecast by the same amount. Sensitivity, knowing which of one's forecasts are the shaky ones, can only come from lessons about kinds of task. That inference is ours, and we know of no experiment that tests it directly.

### The lesson refused

The lesson fails by denial when the learner does not admit the miss. The self-serving bias in attribution is the best-measured form. Mezulis and colleagues (2004), in a meta-analysis of 266 studies, found that people attribute their successes to themselves and their failures to circumstances with an average effect of nearly one standard deviation, which is very large for social psychology, and that the bias is weaker in some cultures than others. A failure put down to bad luck or other people carries no lesson about the task. The inference that self-serving attribution slows learning is ours. The meta-analysis measures the bias and not its cost.

The hypercorrection result shows the other side. When a confident error is met with an accepted correction, learning is at its strongest. The same surprise that teaches most is the one a learner has most reason to explain away.

### Lessons without fresh outcomes

Sleep consolidates memory with no new experience (Diekelmann and Born 2010), and Wagner and colleagues (2004) found that sleep roughly doubled the proportion of people who discovered a hidden shortcut in a task they had practised. Insight of this kind looks like a lesson stroke running offline on outcomes already collected, with a generalisation appearing that lets particulars be discarded. That is a description we find apt and no more. Nothing in those studies measures a lesson as the theory defines one.

## 5. The trust stroke

The fourth stroke is the one that most distinguishes the theory, and the theory makes a double claim about it. Trust that never grows stalls learning. Trust that grows regardless of results stalls learning too. Between them is trust that follows the record, and on its inner side that is calibrated confidence.

### Trust as others' forecast about you

Mayer, Davis and Schoorman (1995) define trust as a willingness to be vulnerable to another party's actions, based on expectations about that party and without the ability to monitor or control them. They trace it to three perceived qualities: ability, benevolence and integrity. Trust on this definition is exposure accepted before the evidence is in, on the strength of a forecast the truster holds about the trusted. The three qualities also mark a limit. A record of scored commitments speaks to ability and integrity and says little about benevolence. Track record is one input to trust and not the whole of it.

The record is kept early in life. Koenig, Clément and Harris (2004) showed three- and four-year-olds one informant who named familiar objects correctly and one who named them wrongly. Children who could say which had been wrong preferred the accurate informant's names for unfamiliar objects. Harris and colleagues (2018) review later work showing that children also weigh what an informant was in a position to know, and sometimes defer for social reasons. Sperber and colleagues (2010) argue that such "epistemic vigilance" is a human universal, and Mercier (2017) that people are in consequence less gullible than commonly supposed, which is an argued position and not a consensus.

Expressed confidence works as a stake in this ledger. Tenney, Spellman and MacCoun (2008) found that observers took a witness's confidence as a sign of accuracy when they had nothing else, and that once they could see how well confidence tracked accuracy, credibility followed calibration. Vullioud and colleagues (2017) put the mechanism in evolutionary terms: confident messages are more readily accepted, and confident speakers pay a higher reputational price when wrong.

### Trust turned inward

Leary and colleagues (1995) proposed that self-esteem is a sociometer, an internal gauge of how far one is included by others. Mahadevan and colleagues (2016) proposed a rival, the hierometer, on which self-regard tracks status, and reported two large cross-sectional studies that favoured it. Both treat self-regard as an internal estimate of external standing. The evidence is largely correlational.

Bandura's (1977) theory of self-efficacy is the closest established relative of the claim that confidence is earned by a record. He named four sources of a person's belief that they can do a thing, and held that the most dependable is having done it: mastery experience. Later evidence supports that half and weakens the other half of his theory, which is that the belief then drives performance. Sitzmann and Yeo (2013), in a meta-analysis of studies following the same people over time, found that past performance predicted later self-efficacy moderately to strongly, while the effect of self-efficacy on later performance was near zero once each person's performance trend was controlled. They concluded that self-efficacy is mainly a product of past performance.

This matters for how the fourth stroke should be stated. The evidence fits the view that inward trust is a reading of the record. It gives little support to the view that raising confidence makes people learn faster. If the fourth stroke bounds learning, the likely route is allocation and not motivation: what the learner attempts next, how much they check, how they weigh their own lessons, and what others let them try.

### Trust that never grows

The theory says a learner whose trust never grows is stalled. The strongest relevant evidence is the revision of learned helplessness. Maier and Seligman (2016) concluded from fifty years of work, much of it on neural circuitry in rats, that the original theory "got it backwards". Passivity under prolonged uncontrollable adversity is the default, produced by a brainstem serotonergic system. What is learned is control: when the prefrontal cortex detects that action changes outcomes, it inhibits that default and can establish an expectation of control that protects against later adversity. Some commentators dispute whether learning has been ruled out of the passive response, and the extension to humans is inferential. If the revision holds, a learner whose acts never visibly move outcomes, or who is never credited when they do, has not lost confidence. They never left the default.

There are two ways for trust not to grow. In one the record is poor and trust is correctly low, which is the stroke working. In the other the record is good and goes uncredited, by others or by the learner. The theory's claim concerns the second. We found no study that isolates it, and so "no credit for what went right stalls learning" stands as conjecture supported by analogy with the helplessness work and by Bandura's account of mastery.

### Trust inflated

Trust that grows regardless is better documented. Moore and Healy (2008) separate overestimating one's performance, overplacing oneself relative to others, and excessive certainty in one's beliefs, and find the third the most persistent. Paulhus and colleagues (2003) measured self-enhancement by asking people how familiar they were with lists of items of which a fifth did not exist. Claiming knowledge of the non-existent items gave an index of self-enhancement that was independent of ability and survived warnings about the foils. Mezulis and colleagues' attribution result belongs here too: credit for the hit, none of the blame for the miss.

Attempts to raise self-belief directly make an instructive case. Growth-mindset interventions tell students that ability can be developed. Sisk and colleagues (2018), in two meta-analyses, found the relation between mindset and achievement weak and the effect of interventions small, with possible benefit for disadvantaged students. Macnamara and Burgoyne (2023) concluded that apparent benefits may be due to weak designs and bias. A large preregistered national experiment (Yeager and colleagues 2019) found a real effect of about a tenth of a grade point. The honest summary is a small effect whose size is disputed. For the theory, the result is what one would expect if inward trust has to be earned by a record and cannot simply be asserted. That reading is ours, and proponents would not accept it.

### Others' trust inflated: status and display

Henrich and Gil-White (2001) distinguished dominance, which is deference taken by force or threat, from prestige, which is deference freely given to those whose skill others want access to. Cheng and colleagues (2013) found that both independently predicted influence in small groups.

Prestige is the part of status that could be a group's running score of whose commitments have paid off. The evidence says the score is kept loosely. Dominance buys rank without accuracy. Prestige follows cues where records are not visible: Jiménez and Mesoudi (2019) note open questions about how well prestige tracks competence, and a later experiment (Brand and colleagues 2020) found that people used prestige cues when direct information about success was unavailable. And overconfidence pays. Anderson and colleagues (2012) found across six studies that people who overrated their ability attained higher status, and that wanting status made people more overconfident. Kennedy, Anderson and Moore (2013) revealed to groups that a member's confidence had been unjustified and reported that the status benefits outweighed the costs.

In the theory's terms the group is performing the fourth stroke by proxy, scoring manner because it cannot see the record, and the proxy can be gamed. Where commitments are made in checkable words and outcomes are visible, the evidence from Tenney and colleagues is that credibility follows calibration. Where they are not, trust follows display.

### One process seen from two sides?

The evidence supports this much: listeners settle confident claims when they can, children keep accuracy records, self-regard moves with standing and self-efficacy with past performance. In each case an inner quantity and an outer one are estimates of how a person's commitments tend to turn out. The evidence does not show that the two estimates agree or that social life pushes them to. Our conclusion is conditional and it is the paper's main conjecture: trust and confidence are one process seen from outside and from inside to the degree that both are driven by the same visible record, and they come apart to the degree that either is running on a proxy.

## 6. Artificial learners

The first three strokes each have a counterpart in machine learning. Much modern training consists of having a system predict something withheld from it and adjusting it by the error, and reinforcement learning is built on the temporal-difference error (Sutton 1988). The standard training loss for classifiers and language models is the logarithmic score, one of the proper scoring rules analysed by Gneiting and Raftery (2007). During training the forecast, the outcome and the update are explicit, and the outcome is one the system cannot author.

Whether the resulting confidence is calibrated is an empirical matter. Guo and colleagues (2017) found that the deep networks of that period were poorly calibrated in a way earlier and smaller networks had not been, and that a simple correction repaired much of the problem. Kadavath and colleagues (2022) reported that large language models were well calibrated on multiple-choice and true-or-false questions in suitable formats, with weaker calibration on unfamiliar tasks. Xiong and colleagues (2024) found that when models stated their confidence in words they were strongly overconfident, with values clustering between 80 and 100 per cent.

The degraded and faked forms of the outcome stroke have direct analogues. Amodei and colleagues (2016) describe reward hacking, in which a system raises its measured score in a way the designer did not intend. Panickssery, Bowman and Feng (2024) found that language models used as evaluators recognised their own outputs at better than chance and rated them more favourably than human judges thought warranted. A system grading its own work, or that of a near copy, is self-scoring, with the limits section 3 describes.

What follows is speculation. A deployed system that does not change its parameters learns, if at all, through what is placed in front of it when it acts. For such a system the lesson stroke would be something written into future context, and the fourth stroke would be the scope it is granted. The theory predicts that such a system stalls for the same reasons a person does: forecasts never stated, outcomes it scores itself, lessons about itself in place of the task, and scope that is fixed whatever its record or widened regardless of it. It also predicts that a lesson worth keeping survives replacement of the underlying model, since one that does not was a fact about the occupant and not about the work. We know of no published test.

Whether such a system could develop something deserving the names confidence or trust is unclear. The functional question is tractable: whether its stated uncertainty is usable by those who depend on it, and whether the latitude it is given follows its record. Whether anything is felt by a system as its record improves is a question this paper cannot address.

## 7. Is the reframed theory empirical?

### The objection

An earlier draft of this paper stated the theory as a requirement for four explicit phases in a fixed order and found it false: retrieval practice, statistical learning and sleep consolidation all proceed without them. The theory as now stated says the strokes may be implicit. The danger is obvious. If any internal expectation counts as a forecast, any later input as an outcome, any change as a lesson and any persistence as trust, then every case of learning has four strokes by definition, and every stall has a missing one by definition, and the theory forbids nothing.

Three things keep it from that condition, and we do not think they fully succeed.

### An evidential line for implicit strokes

The first is a rule about what may be claimed. An implicit stroke counts only where there is evidence for it that is independent of the learning it is supposed to explain. By that rule the implicit forecast is in good standing in several cases: dopamine responses to omitted rewards, infant cortical responses to omitted stimuli, infants' selective exploration of objects that violated expectation. Observational learning is another. Burke and colleagues (2010) found, in people learning by watching another player, brain activity corresponding to separate errors for the other's action and the other's outcome. The learner profits from a forecast they did not publicly make because they made one privately.

Other implicit strokes are posited and not evidenced, and should be labelled so. Statistical learning is the clearest case. Saffran, Aslin and Newport (1996) showed that eight-month-olds, after two minutes of a continuous syllable stream, distinguished sequences that had reliably occurred together from those that had not. Reber (1967) showed that adults who memorised letter strings generated by hidden rules could classify new strings above chance without being able to state the rules. It is plausible that these learners predict the next element and are corrected by its arrival, and the infant omission result makes it more plausible. An implicit lesson is also arguable, since what is acquired generalises to new strings. An implicit fourth stroke here, some internal weighting of how far to rely on what has been learned, is posited to save the theory. We know of no measurement of it in these paradigms and we concede the stretch.

Retrieval practice without feedback is the hard case for the outcome stroke. The reply available is that the outcome is retrieval itself: the learner finds out whether the answer came. Rowland's (2014) meta-analysis treats as viable an account on which the benefit of testing falls on the items successfully retrieved, and secondary summaries of the same analysis report larger effects when feedback is given. Both fit a picture in which an internal outcome does some of the work and an external one does more. But success at retrieval is a signal the learner generates, and counting it as an outcome relaxes the rule that the outcome is not self-authored. The safer statement is that strengthening access to what one already knows is a kind of learning for which the outcome stroke is weak or internal, and the theory covers it only loosely.

Prepared and one-shot learning mark a boundary. Rats readily associate taste with illness and sound with shock and not the reverse (Garcia and Koelling 1966), and people can learn a visual concept from one example, which Lake, Salakhutdinov and Tenenbaum (2015) modelled with rich prior structure. The theory describes the rate of learning from experience and says nothing about what the learner brings.

### Predictions that could fail

The second safeguard is that the bottleneck structure makes predictions a checklist would not. It predicts interactions. Improving a stroke should speed learning in proportion to how poor that stroke was, and should do little where it was already good. Four cases follow.

Replacing a remembered forecast with a recorded one should help most where hindsight distortion is strongest and little where expectations are already stable and explicit. Replacing self-scoring with an external scorer should help most on complex, unfamiliar tasks, where Zell and Krizan found self-assessment weakest, and little on simple objective ones. Adding feedback to retrieval practice should help most where initial retrieval often fails. Replacing self-level feedback with task-level feedback should improve the sensitivity of later confidence and not only its bias. And where records of explicit commitments are made visible to a group, the status premium for displayed confidence should shrink.

Rival accounts differ. A motivation account predicts that enjoyment and learning rise together, where this theory predicts they separate whenever the outcome is self-scored, as Deslauriers and colleagues found. A pure prediction-error account covers the first two strokes and is silent on lessons and on trust.

### What would count against it

The third safeguard is to name the observations that would embarrass the theory. It is in trouble if interventions on a stroke produce uniform gains whatever the stroke's prior quality, since the bottleneck claim is then idle. It is in trouble if learners can be found whose four strokes all measure as good and whose learning has nonetheless stalled, or learners in whom a stroke is demonstrably absent, and not merely unobserved, who learn at full rate. It is in trouble if people who take credit regardless of results learn as fast as calibrated people given the same feedback. The Sitzmann and Yeo result already presses on the fourth stroke: if confidence is mostly a readout of performance with little forward effect, the stroke may be a consequence of learning and not one of its conditions. The lesson stroke is idle if task-level lessons add nothing to scored feedback alone. And the primacy of the outcome fails if, in the crossed experiment described in section 3, degrading the forecast or the lesson costs as much as replacing an external outcome with a self-scored one.

All of these tests need the quality of each stroke to be measured independently of the learning rate, or the diagnosis is circular. Candidate measures exist. For the forecast there is whether one was recorded, and neural or behavioural signs of expectation. For the outcome there is the delay and validity of feedback and the correlation between self-assessment and performance. For the lesson there is whether attributions are to task or to self and whether the next forecast changes. For trust there is the calibration of confidence and whether granted latitude tracks record. No study we found measures all four on the same learners.

Our assessment is that the reframed theory is empirical in its bottleneck predictions, well supported for the first two strokes, and still partly protected by its own flexibility for the third and fourth. It is safest to hold it as a diagnostic heuristic with testable parts, and not as a law.

## 8. A diagnostic for stalled learning

For a learner or a team whose progress has stopped, the theory offers four questions. They are not stages to be walked through, since the strokes run together. They are ordered by where a fault is most likely to be decisive, and so they begin with the outcome. It is worth stopping at the first clear failure, because the weakest stroke is where effort pays.

The first question is who or what says how it went, because nothing downstream can add information the outcome did not carry. If the answer is the learner, ask how good a judge they can be of this kind of work. On concrete, familiar tasks self-scoring may serve. On complex or new ones it is the likely bottleneck, and the change is to find a verifier the learner cannot influence: a test, a measurement, a customer's behaviour, a colleague who does not share the learner's blind spots. If there is an outside verifier, ask whether it is prompt, specific and hard to flatter. Feedback that arrives months later, or that rewards the appearance of the thing, is a degraded stroke. A scorer whose rubric the learner can read and write toward needs particular care. If the environment simply does not return outcomes that bear on the skill, experience there will build confidence and not competence, and the honest response is to practise somewhere kinder or to trust intuition less.

The second question is what was expected, and whether it was fixed before the result was known. If nobody can produce what they thought would happen before it happened, the forecast is being remembered and not recorded, and memory will have been kind. A good outcome is then wasted, because there is nothing for it to contradict. The change is to write the expectation down before the attempt, with a rough confidence and the observation that would settle it. If people are unwilling to do this, the problem is the cost of being wrong in that setting and not the people. Forecasts get offered where a miss is treated as information. The change then belongs to whoever sets the tone: reward the calibrated miss, and stop rewarding the vague confident manner that cannot be checked.

The third question is what was taken from the last surprise, and whether it was about the task. If misses are explained by luck, by other people or by circumstances, no lesson is being drawn. If they are explained by a trait, such as carelessness or being bad at this, a lesson is being drawn at the level where it does least good. The change is to write, after a surprise, one sentence about the kind of situation in which the forecast failed, tied to the cases behind it, and to read it before the next attempt of that kind. A short list that is consulted beats a long one that is filed.

The fourth question is whether confidence and latitude have moved with the record. There are two failures and they call for opposite remedies. If the record is good and nothing has grown, with the learner still checked as closely as on the first day or still doubting what they have repeatedly got right, the change is to show them the record and extend the room it warrants. If confidence or standing has grown without the record, with credit taken for hits and misses explained away, the change is again to show the record, and to let scope follow it down as well as up. In both cases the remedy is that the record be visible to the person it concerns and to those who rely on them. A good record should mean being checked after the act and not before it. It should not mean going unchecked.

The same questions apply to teaching, where they recommend marking by someone other than the student, prediction before instruction, feedback aimed at the work, and credit that follows demonstrated performance. They apply to tools, which should return what happened against what was said and hold nothing about the user that the user cannot read. And they apply to institutions, which are typically rich in commitments nobody scores and in scores that change nothing.

## 9. Conclusion

Stated as a requirement for four explicit phases, the theory is false. Stated as a diagnostic it fares better. The claim is that the four strokes are in effect wherever learning works, running together and not in turn, in forms that may be implicit, partial or by proxy; that the weakest of them sets the pace; and that the outcome, as the only stroke the learner cannot supply from within, matters most. The one order that binds is that a forecast be fixed before its outcome is known. The first two strokes rest on secure findings: surprise teaches, an expectation committed in advance is what makes surprise available, hindsight removes it, and self-scoring is a weak substitute for an independent outcome. The third has indirect support and no direct test. The primacy of the outcome fits what is known about feedback, learning environments and self-assessment, and has not been tested by a direct comparison of the strokes. The fourth stroke is the theory's distinctive contribution and its most exposed part. Confidence does appear to be a reading of one's record, trust a forecast others hold, and agency something built from outcomes that answer to one's acts. But the evidence that inward trust drives learning forward is thin, and groups routinely extend trust on display.

The reframing buys coverage at a price. It can absorb retrieval practice and statistical learning only by positing strokes that have not all been measured, and we have tried to say where. What keeps it useful is that it tells a stalled learner where to look, and predicts that the repair will help in proportion to how weak the stroke was. That prediction can be tested and has not yet been.

### A note on verification

Every entry above was checked against at least one fetched source for authors, year, venue and main finding. Most were checked at the level of abstracts, publisher or index records and secondary summaries, not full text. Where a finding reported in the body rests on secondary description only, the body or the entry says so. This applies in particular to Tetlock (2005), Boyd (1996), Osinga (2007), the effect sizes attributed to Rowland (2014), and the account of Hogarth's earlier description of wicked environments.
